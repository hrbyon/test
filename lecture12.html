Mixed Potential Bench

Two reactions on one piece of metal, and the metal cannot be at either equilibrium: it settles where their rates match, and that potential tells you nothing at all about how fast it is being eaten. Build the Evans diagram, read the corrosion rate off it, and move the lines the way a corrosion engineer does.

Two reactions, one piece of metal, and a potential that belongs to neither

mixed potential, corrosion, and the Evans diagram
Σ ianodic = Σ icathodic  ⇒   Aa ia(Ecorr) = Ac ic(Ecorr) the only condition — charge in equals charge out, as current, not as current density
ia = i0,M · 10(E − EM)/ba  ·   ic = i0,X · 10−(E − EX)/bc each branch is just Tafel, from Lecture 10
icorr = the common value where they meet   ·   rate = icorrM/zMFρ Faraday’s law as a penetration rate — on steel, 1 µA/cm2 is 11.6 µm/year
icorr = B/Rp,   B = babc/2.303(ba+bc) Stern–Geary: the slope through Ecorr gives the rate without removing the sample. B = 17.4 mV for b = 60/120 mV

Zinc in acid: Zn/Zn2+ wants −0.76 V, H+/H2 wants 0 V, and one conductor cannot be at both. It settles in between, where neither couple is at equilibrium: zinc dissolves steadily and hydrogen comes off at the matching rate. That is the mixed potential — Ecorr when it is corrosion, at the rate icorr.

Three consequences. It is not an equilibrium: two rates set it, so it depends on kinetics, not on Nernst. No net current leaves, so a steady open-circuit reading says nothing about how fast the metal is going. And the two reactions need not share a place — which is where the areas start doing the damage.

The Evans diagram

—
Anodic line — the metal dissolving. Slope ba, drawn from its own (EM, i0,M) — the dot its name sits on. Cathodic line — the oxidant that is actually carrying the cathodic current, named at its own dot. Where it turns vertical it has hit its transport limit; oxygen almost always does, and then diffusion alone sets icorr. the crossing is (icorr, Ecorr). Both lines are currents, scaled by area — so the area ratio alone slides it. the V is what a potentiostat measures: |net current| swept through Ecorr, diving to zero where the two cancel. Extrapolating its legs back recovers icorr — Tafel extrapolation. Faint lines are the branches that are not doing the work — the metal's own cathodic leg, an oxidant's anodic leg, a second oxidant that loses. They are drawn so that you can see why they lose: they sit to the left of the winner at every potential. Purple line appears only with a sacrificial anode: a second, more active metal added in parallel. It drags Ecorr down to near its own E, where the protected metal's anodic current is negligible.
