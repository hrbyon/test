Potential Step Bench

Hold the potential still and watch the clock instead. Before anything else: the potential you set is not the one the electrode gets, because the current has to cross the solution to reach it. Then two currents come back at once — one charging the double layer and dying exponentially, one carrying the reaction and dying as 1/√t — and almost everything in this lecture is the window between them where only the second is left. Shrink the electrode until that window never closes, and pulse the potential until the first current never appears.

Why nothing is clean before 5τ

the first few τ magnified, then every decade at once

Read the top panel first. Its clock is measured in τ, so the charging curve never moves and the sliders slide the faradaic one across it. Up to about 5τ the thin grey line — the only thing an instrument ever sees — is the charging current with the chemistry buried inside it; after 5τ the grey line and the green one are the same curve. The panel below is the same race over every decade there is, which is where you can see that the crossing is not a coincidence.

The two currents die at different speeds, and that is what makes the experiment possible. ic falls exponentially, e−t/τ; the faradaic current falls only as t−1/2 (Cottrell). An exponential beats any power law eventually, so there is always a time after which the charging current has gone and the faradaic one has not — and it arrives at about 5τ. Before that the measured current is mostly the cell charging itself and no analysis of it means anything; after it, what is left is chemistry. This is why pulse methods sample late instead of integrating, and why shrinking τ — a smaller electrode, more supporting electrolyte, a Luggin tip — buys earlier access to the reaction.

What a step actually looks like at the electrode

the circuit, then what it does

τ is how long the cell takes to obey you. The step has to charge Cdl through Ru, so the electrode arrives at E(1 − e−t/τ) rather than at once, and what is still missing is exactly iRu. One τ leaves 37 % of the step undelivered, three leave 5 %, five leave 0.67 % — which is where the habit of discarding everything before 5τ comes from. The faradaic branch changes none of this: it is in parallel with Cdl, so at t = 0 the capacitor is a short circuit and the whole step still falls across Ru.

τ = RuCdl the time constant of the cell — an ohm times a farad is a second. It is how long the step takes to charge the double layer through the solution resistance, and it is the only time constant this circuit has.
what the electrode got = E(1 − e−t/τ) the potential the interface has actually reached at time t — the green curve. You command E; the electrode arrives at it, and only after a few τ.
iRu = E − E(electrode) the gap between the two curves, at every instant: the current of that instant times the uncompensated resistance. It is the error in your potential axis, and it is zero only when the current is.
5τ 99.3 % of the step delivered, so 0.67 % still missing — the earliest time anything measured here means what you think it means. One τ leaves 37 %, three leave 5 %.

Three things the third electrode cannot do

It splits the two jobs, and that is all. With two electrodes, one of them both carries the current and acts as the reference, so the reading contains its interface, its overpotential and the whole solution resistance. The third electrode moves the current into a separate loop and leaves the reference drawing nothing, so its own interface stays undisturbed. What survives is Ru — the slice of solution between the working electrode and the reference tip, which is in the measurement loop by construction. The potentiostat cannot know about it, because the drop happens outside the point where it measures.

And moving the tip closer stops helping almost at once. Current funnels into a disc from every direction, so the lines are crowded right at the electrode and spread out immediately: most of the resistance is in the first radius. The curve above is (1/4κa)[1−(2/π)arctan(a/d)], and at exactly one radius you carry exactly half of it — arctan(1) = π/4 makes that exact, for any electrode and any electrolyte. A Luggin capillary is worth building, but it buys a factor, not an order of magnitude — and pushing it closer than about a tenth of a radius starts shielding the electrode, which distorts the current distribution instead. The term that actually moves Ru is κ, and κ is supporting electrolyte.

It shows up differently in every technique on this page. In a step it is a delay: the electrode charges through Ru with τ = RuCdl, so the potential it actually sees creeps up over several τ and nothing before 5τ means anything — that is the whole near end of the next tab. In a sweep it is a stretch along the potential axis, proportional to current, so peaks move apart and a reversible couple reads as a sluggish one. In a pulse it sets how long you must wait before sampling. Every number on the next four tabs has Ru inside it.

Getting a number for D out of this

the slope, and where to put the window
slope m = nFAD1/2C*/√π what the fitted line gives you — i against t−1/2 is a straight line through the origin, and its slope is the only thing you measure.
D = (m√π / nFAC*)2 the answer, rearranged. Note the square: every error in the slope, in A, or in C* is doubled in D. A 10 % slope is a 21 % D.

The recipe. Step to a potential well past E0′ — far enough that the surface concentration is pinned at zero and the current is diffusion-limited, which is the only condition under which Cottrell holds. Sample the current over a window that starts after 5τ and ends before the shorter of tedge = r02/2D, where the disc stops being planar, and roughly 20 s, where the room starts stirring the solution. Plot i against t−1/2, fit, and read the slope. Then look at the intercept before you look at the slope.

i against t−1/2

the diagnostic plot

What this plot is for. Its slope carries D, since everything else in nFAD½C*/√π is known; and the Cottrell equation has no constant term, so an intercept that is not zero says the data are not Cottrell, whatever R² says. Sampling too early lifts the right-hand end (large t−1/2) and steepens the line; sampling too late lifts the left-hand end and flattens it while pushing the intercept up. D goes as the slope squared, so every error is doubled.

And the five ways the number comes out wrong

read the intercept before you read the slope

Why the intercept is the real test. A true Cottrell plot passes through the origin, because i → 0 as t → ∞. A finite intercept means something in your current does not decay as t−1/2: leftover charging, a background reaction, an adsorbed layer stripping, or convection holding the current up at long times. R2 will not catch it — 1/√t crushes the late points together, so a plot can be visibly curved and still fit at R2 = 0.999. Drag the window and watch D move: if D depends on where you put the window, the window is in the wrong place, not D.

too early D reads high. Residual ic sits on top of the first points, which are also the ones with the largest t−1/2, so they lever the slope up.
too late D reads high as well, for the opposite reason. Convection, or radial diffusion at the edge of the disc, holds the current above the Cottrell line; the late points lift and the line pivots.
the area A the usual culprit. A is the electroactive area, not what the calipers say. A roughness factor of 1.3 taken as 1.0 puts D out by 69 %. Calibrate A first with a couple whose D you already trust — that is what ferrocyanide is for.
n and D together one slope cannot separate them. Cottrell measures nD1/2; you must know n from somewhere else (coulometry, the wave shape, the chemistry) before D means anything.
C* and the surface both have to be what you assumed. C* is the bulk concentration, so the solution must be unstirred and the electrode must start from a clean, fully relaxed state — a step taken too soon after the last one meets solution that is still depleted, and reads low.

And report it honestly: D depends on temperature by about 2 % per kelvin and on the supporting electrolyte through viscosity, so a D without a stated temperature and medium is not a measurement. Quote the window you fitted over, the intercept, and how A was obtained — those three decide the number far more than the regression does.

How each waveform does it

what is subtracted from what, and why

The one move all three make. The charging current dies exponentially with its own time constant τ = RuCdl, while the Faradaic current only falls as t−1/2. So do not integrate the pulse — sample it late. At tm = 5τ the charging current is down to e−5 = 0.7 % of what it started at, while the Faradaic current has lost only a factor √5. At 10τ it is 4.5×10−3 %. Waiting is almost free, and everything below is bookkeeping on top of that one fact — what differs between the three methods is only how many times you sample, and what you subtract from what.

sampled at the end of the pulse — the forward sample, in all three methods. Late enough that ic has gone. sampled just before the pulse — DPV only. This is the sample that carries the background away with it. sampled at the end of the reverse half — SWV only. This current is anodic, which is why subtracting it makes the peak bigger and not smaller.

NPV — one sample, and the solution is new every time. The base potential is held somewhere nothing reacts. Each cycle steps to a slightly more extreme potential, holds, samples once near the end of the pulse, and goes back to base. That return is the point: during the long base period the product diffuses away and the concentration profile relaxes, so every pulse meets essentially unperturbed solution. Each point is therefore an independent potential-step experiment at its own potential, and what you sample is the Cottrell current at tm scaled by the Nernstian fraction reduced, i = id/(1 + θ). The result is a sigmoid — a polarogram without the mercury drop — with the plateau at id = nFAC*√(D/πtm). You read E1/2 off the half-height and id off the plateau. What you do not get is any cancellation: the charging current here is only waited out, never removed, so the floor is set entirely by how completely e−tm/τ has died.

DPV — two samples, and their difference is a derivative. The base is now a staircase that walks through the wave, with a small fixed pulse ΔE (10–50 mV) riding on each step. Sample twice: once just before the pulse, once at its end, and plot the difference. Because those two samples sit at E and E+ΔE on the same sigmoid, Δi is a finite-difference derivative of it — and the derivative of a sigmoid is a peak. That is the whole reason DPV gives a peak where NPV gives a step, and it puts the peak at the midpoint of the two sampling potentials, Ep = E1/2 − ΔE/2. Let ΔE → 0 and the peak approaches the true derivative: the width falls to 3.52RT/nF = 90.4/n mV, but the height falls linearly with ΔE. That trade is the whole of DPV method development. And the sensitivity comes from the subtraction, not the waiting: both samples carry nearly the same residual charging current and the same background, so differencing cancels them. That is what reaches 10−8 M, which no amount of waiting alone would.

SWV — two samples that add instead of cancelling. A symmetric square wave of amplitude Esw sits on the staircase, so every cycle has a forward half and a reverse half, each sampled at its end, and what is plotted is Δi = if − ir. The forward pulse makes product right at the electrode, and the reverse pulse immediately re-oxidises it, so ir is anodic — opposite in sign. Subtracting it therefore adds. Both peak heights have closed forms — DPV is tanh(nfΔE/4) and SWV is tanh(nfEsw/2) — so the ratio is exactly 2 only in the small-pulse limit, and it falls away as the pulse grows: 1.98 at 10 mV, 1.89 at 25 mV, 1.66 at 50 mV. “Twice the DPV peak” is a statement about small pulses, not a constant. Because the square wave is symmetric about each step, the net peak sits at E1/2, with none of the half-pulse offset DPV carries. And since the reverse half uses what the forward half just made, instead of needing it to be gone, nothing has to wait for the diffusion layer to relax: the scan runs at 10–1000 Hz, so what takes DPV minutes takes SWV a second. Keep if and ir separately and you get something neither of the others offers — a reversible couple gives a large reverse component, while an irreversible one, or one with a fast following reaction, gives almost none and Δi collapses onto the forward curve alone. That is the CV return peak, in a pulse experiment.

Why this is the tab an analyst lives in. Cyclic voltammetry is a mechanism instrument: it is read for shapes and shifts. The pulse methods are measurement instruments, and all three exist for one reason — the charging current is what stands between you and a low detection limit. NPV removes none of it and is read for E1/2 and id; DPV differences it away and is read for concentration; SWV differences it away as well, gets twice the signal for the trouble, and is read for concentration and for whether the couple is reversible. Not one of the three alters the electrochemistry — they only change when you look.
