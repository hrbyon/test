Overpotential Bench

Thermodynamics says what a cell is worth; nothing in it says how fast. The moment current flows you get less — an activation barrier, a depleted layer, the resistance of the solution. Take the three apart, then put them back together in a fuel cell and an electrolyser.

Ten orders of magnitude for one reaction — and ΔG is identical all the way down: H+/H2 is 0 V vs SHE on every one. Only the surface differs. Pt is best for HER and HOR because its M–H bond sits at the top of the volcano: strong enough to adsorb, weak enough to let go.

Read the bottom the other way and it is just as useful. Hg and Pb are hopeless at making hydrogen, which is why they open a window past −2 V in water — and why a zinc battery does not fizz away (Lecture 11).

What both cells obey

one layer, one ceiling, one cost
j = nFD (c* − cs)/δ   ·   jL = nFDc*/δ at cs = 0   ·   ηconc = (RT/nF) ln(1 − j/jL)
Fick’s first law across a layer made linear  ·  the ceiling it sets  ·  what approaching that ceiling costs

δ — the Nernst diffusion layer, in cm. The slab of solution against the electrode that is treated as stagnant, so that transport across it is pure diffusion and the profile through it is a straight line; beyond δ the solution is taken to be perfectly mixed at c*. No such boundary exists: δ is defined as the distance at which the tangent to the real profile at the surface reaches the bulk concentration, which is exactly what makes the straight-line picture give the right current. Typically a few hundred µm in a quiet cell and ten-odd µm on a spinning disc.

jL — the limiting current density, in A/cm2. The most current the solution can deliver. Push harder and cs falls; once it hits zero the gradient is as steep as it can be, c*/δ, and no potential will raise the current any further — the extra drive goes into ηconc, or into some other reaction. Since jL ∝ c*/δ, there are only two ways to lift the ceiling: more reactant, or a thinner layer.

Convection carries, diffusion does only the last step. That makes δ calculable rather than estimated: you choose δ by choosing ω. Replay the step and the disc has a transient too: δ grows as √(πDt), exactly as in the still cell, until the flow pins it at the Levich thickness. The difference is when — milliseconds here, never there. The Levich test. jL against √ω straight through the origin = genuinely transport-limited, slope nD2/3c*. Bent, or missing the origin, = kinetics or a film.

How to read it

three uses and one trap

1. Different questions. A still cell gives a transient whose shape carries D and c*; a disc gives a plateau whose height does, at a δ you chose. Only the disc holds a steady current — which is what every amperometric sensor is.

2. Stirring is a lever on jL alone. Quiescent (δ ≈ 500 µm) to 2000 rpm (δ = 11.1 µm) is 45× more current, and not one millivolt of ηact: diffusion does not know what the electrode is made of.

The trap. A curve that bends over is not proof of a transport limit — iR and a growing film bend it the same way. They separate: transport scales with √ω, iR with 1/κ, a film with time.

A real cell loses all three at once

deck p.10, p.15–17 — the water electrolyser and the PEM fuel cell
galvanic (it gives you work): V(i) = Erev − balog(i/i0,a) − bclog(i/i0,c) − iRarea + (RT/nF)ln(1 − i/iL)
electrolytic (you pay work into it): V(i) = Erev + balog(i/i0,a) + bclog(i/i0,c) + iRarea − (RT/nF)ln(1 − i/iL)

Why the last term alone flips the other way. The first three are written as positive quantities — b log(i/i0) > 0 once i > i0, and iRarea > 0 — so they are subtracted from a galvanic cell and added to an electrolytic one. The last is written as the signed Nernst shift, and (RT/nF)ln(1 − i/iL) is negative for every 0 < i < iL, because the logarithm of a number below 1 is negative. So it needs the opposite operator to mean the same thing: + in the galvanic line and − in the electrolytic one, both of which cost you |ηconc|. The page computes it with the sign already taken out, ηconc = −(RT/ncF)ln(1 − i/iL) > 0, and then subtracts all four from Erev or adds all four to it.

Same three terms, opposite signs. A fuel cell worth 1.23 V delivers less; an electrolyser needing 1.23 V is fed more; both differences are overpotential, wasted as heat. Round-trip efficiency is the ratio of the two, which is why these terms are the whole engineering problem.

They dominate in order as the current rises, and each gives the curve its own shape: activation, a steep logarithmic drop out of open circuit; ohmic, a straight line; concentration, a cliff. Which region you are in says what to fix — catalyst, membrane, or flow field.

Where the voltage goes

Read it downward. Top bar = what thermodynamics promises; each bar below is one term taking its cut; bottom bar = what the cell delivers, or what an electrolyser demands. The percentage is voltage efficiency Vwork/Erev, not energy efficiency. Past iL the bars say so. The concentration row is drawn full width and marked → ∞, and the bottom row reads → −∞ with “nothing — the cell is being driven”. There is no working voltage at that current, so the efficiency has no value either — the readouts beside the plot show em dashes rather than numbers. All bars to one scale, so the largest cut is obvious. Slide the current: activation barely moves (logarithmic), ohmic grows in proportion, concentration is nothing at all until it is everything.

The polarisation curve, and how to read it

V against i, with each loss labelled
Reading a loss off it. Pick a current and measure from the dashed Erev line to the curve: that gap is everything you lost. For a cell the curve runs below that line and the brackets hang down from it; for an electrolyser it runs above and they stack up. Either way the brackets split the gap into ηact, ηΩ, ηconc — the waterfall’s numbers, drawn in place, each band exactly as long as the number beside it. If ηconc has run away, the other two still carry numbers: they are what activation and iR would cost at that current. Raise iL past it and all three are finite again. What ηconc is, and when it stops being a number. Below the limit it is the voltage the depleted surface costs you, (RT/nF)ln(1 − i/iL): the reactant arrives more slowly than you are consuming it, cs falls below c*, and Nernst charges you for the difference. At i = iL the surface is empty, cs = 0, and the logarithm diverges — so it is not a large loss but a wall: no potential makes the reactant arrive any faster, the cell has no steady voltage at that current, and the extra current a real cell is asked for goes into some other reaction — in water, breaking up the solvent. When the losses beat Erev. Draw a large current through a large Ru and the three overpotentials together exceed Erev: the curve crosses the grey dotted V = 0 line, the voltage axis opens downward, and the cell delivers nothing — it is being driven instead. Near jL the voltage runs away altogether and the curve simply leaves the frame.

Switch the current on and watch the clock

one point of the curve above, taken apart in time
This is the violet point on the polarisation curve above, at the same current, with time on the axis instead of current. Hold the current constant from t = 0 and record the voltage: each loss has its own clock, and the gap the brackets measure up there is what this curve ends at on the right. ηΩ is a step: Ohm’s law has nothing to charge or deplete. Interrupt the current and whatever vanishes instantly is the iR drop — that is current-interrupt compensation. ηact in microseconds, the time the double layer needs to charge through Ru — RuCd. Chemically instant too, hence the confusion; only one of the two survives an interruption. ηconc in tens of seconds, as δ = √(πDt) thickens, stopping only when convection pins δ. Above jL it never stops. The double-layer transient is stretched to be visible — the real one is four to six decades faster.

How to read it

the four numbers that matter in a real device

1. One electrode owns the activation loss. HOR on Pt has j0 ≈ 10−3 A/cm2, so the anode is free. ORR on the same metal is 10−9 to 10−6 and alone takes 300–400 mV. Pt is best for HER/HOR and poor for ORR/OER — six orders of magnitude on one piece of platinum, which is why catalysis is an industry.

2. Ohmic loss is a thickness. A good PEM is ~0.1 Ω cm2, i.e. 100 mV at 1 A/cm2; halving the membrane halves it, until hydrogen starts crossing. Alkaline gaps run 0.3–0.5 Ω cm2, which is why they run at lower current.

3. The transport cliff is a flooding problem. A gas-fed electrode is limited by gas reaching the catalyst through the porous layer, and that collapses when water fills the pores — a water-management failure, not a kinetic one.

4. The ceiling moves too. Erev is 1.229 V for liquid water at 25 °C, 1.18 V for steam, and falls with temperature and partial pressure. None of that is overpotential: keep the two columns separate.
