The sliders are labelled as concentrations, but the quantity the equation actually wants is the activity. See the note below.

Why a solid drops out

A pure solid or a pure liquid has activity 1 by definition — there is no "concentration of solid zinc" to change. So for a plating couple only the dissolved species appears in the log term, and the slider for the solid is switched off.

What the temperature slider does not do

Moving T here changes only the slope, 2.303RT/nF. Every tab on this page holds E° fixed while you do it, which is not true of a real couple: E° carries its own temperature dependence, dE°/dT = ΔS°/nF, because the entropy of the reaction is not zero. Treat the temperature slider as showing you the log term's sensitivity, not a real thermal scan.

Activities, not concentrations

what the log term really contains
The Nernst equation is written in terms of activities: E = E° + (2.303RT/nF) log(aO/aR), where a = γ·c/c° and γ is the activity coefficient. On this page, activities are approximated by concentrations — that is, γ ≈ 1 — purely for simplicity. That approximation is decent in dilute solution and gets worse as the solution becomes concentrated or the ions more highly charged, where γ can fall well below 1 and shift E by tens of millivolts. So read every number here as an idealised value, not a prediction for a real 1 M solution.

Why this cell works at all

Both electrodes are the same metal in the same salt, so the two E° values are identical and E°cell = 0 exactly. Every millivolt on the voltmeter comes from the log term and nothing else — this is the cleanest possible demonstration that the Nernst term is real. The driving force is not chemistry but mixing: the cell runs until the two concentrations meet, then stops dead.

η is not a potential in its own right — it is the distance from equilibrium, and it only exists relative to a couple. At η = 0 the interface is not idle: oxidation and reduction both run, at equal rates, so nothing appears in the wire. Making η negative raises EF above the couple's level and reduction outruns oxidation; making it positive does the reverse.

Its size sets the rate. The faradaic current grows by roughly a decade for every 120 mV of η, which is why the useful range is a few hundred millivolts wide rather than a few volts — and why the counter electrode in Lecture 3 needs its own η to carry the same current.
