CH40051 Electroanalytical Chemistry · Lecture 15 · Pt catalysis and electrochemically active surface area

ECSA Bench

A catalyst’s activity is a current divided by an area — and the area is the hard part. The geometric area is not it, the BET area is not it, and what you want is the number of atoms the electrolyte can actually reach. On platinum you can count them: one hydrogen adsorbs on each, one electron each, so a charge becomes an atom count and an atom count becomes square centimetres. Here is that voltammogram, built from the surface waves rather than traced, with the integral in your hands — then the same question asked with carbon monoxide, and then asked on a material that has nothing to adsorb at all.

One cycle, four things happening to one layer of atoms

and only the first of them counts the atoms

Up through 0.05 → 0.4 V: the hydrogen comes off. The surface starts saturated — one H per Pt, put there below 0.4 V at a potential above where bulk H2 would form, which is what underpotential means. The sweep strips it in two waves because the weakly and the strongly bound sites let go at different potentials. One electron leaves per atom, so the charge under this region is an atom count and nothing else. This is the only part of the cycle you can turn into an area.

0.4 → 0.8 V: nothing reacts. The metal is bare. A few anions from the electrolyte sit on it as spectators, and the current is the double layer charging alone — a flat box of υCdlA that does not move when you change the potential, only when you change the sweep rate. That flat box is the baseline every integration on this page depends on, and it is the only place in the cycle where you can honestly read it.

0.8 V and up: water is discharged onto the same sites. OH goes down where H used to be, and with enough potential and enough time it goes on to PtO and deeper. That is why the oxide charge is not an area: the number of electrons per surface atom depends on how far you swept and how long you waited, so there is no constant to divide by. The further you go, the more the surface rearranges when you strip it off again — the electrode you measure on the tenth cycle is rougher than the one you started with.

Back down: the same film, taken off in a different shape. The oxide comes off in one sharper peak near 0.78 V rather than over the broad range it grew across, which is the signature of a reduction that only starts once it is driven; then the bare double layer again, and then hydrogen re-adsorbing into the same two waves it left from. Qads and Qdes should agree, and their average is what the next tab divides by 210 µC cm−2.

Why the number is quoted to two figures

The baseline is a guess, and it is the largest term you control. The double-layer line is read off 0.4–0.6 V, where the Pt is bare, and then extended back under the Hupd peaks, where it is covered in hydrogen — a different surface with a different capacitance. Nothing in the trace tells you how different. Drag the baseline across its range above and watch a number that looked like a measurement move by tens of percent without the voltammogram changing shape at all.

The window loses charge at one end and invents it at the other. The weakly bound peak runs down under the negative limit, so a window that stops at 0.05 V never collects all of it — the readout above says how much. Push the limit down to recover the tail and hydrogen evolution comes up to meet you, adding charge that was never on the surface. These two errors have opposite signs, which is why a sloppy integration can look accurate; they do not cancel reliably, and the one that wins depends on the catalyst. Averaging Qads and Qdes helps, because HER contaminates the cathodic sweep more than the anodic one.

And then there is the constant. 210 and 197 µC cm−2 differ by 6.6 %, and switching between them with the pills above changes every area reported from this electrode by that much, with no new measurement and no indication in the data. The deck's slide on the facet densities derives both; the supplement behind it shows that the same slide also pairs 210 with the 2.04 nmol cm−2 that belongs to 197. State which constant you used. Most of the disagreement between two papers on the same catalyst is here, in these three choices, and not in the catalyst.

The same atoms, counted with carbon monoxide instead

a second count is the only check the first one has

Everything above divides a charge by a charge per square centimetre, and every one of the ways it goes wrong — a baseline that is a guess, a window that clips the monolayer, a constant that is a convention — leaves the voltammogram looking exactly the same. The only way to catch it is to count the same atoms a second time by a different route. CO is that route: one monolayer you put there on purpose, oxidised off in one sharp peak that sits by itself. Where the two counts agree, both assumptions held; where they disagree, one of them broke, and the first thing to check is whether Hupd was fully suppressed in the first cycle.

A monolayer you put there on purpose

and two electrons for every molecule of it
CO + H2O → CO2 + 2H+ + 2e−  ·   ECSA = QCO / (2 × 210 µC cm−2) two electrons per CO, so one CO on every Pt atom strips at 420 µC cm−2
bridged CO: one molecule, two sites  →   Q = 420(1 − x/2) µC cm−2 x is the fraction of sites bridged, and no part of the voltammogram tells you what it is

QCO is the difference between the two cycles. The first scan grows Pt oxide over the same window as the CO peak — here that is tens of microcoulombs, comparable with the strip itself. Subtracting the second, CO-free cycle cancels the oxide and the double layer exactly and leaves only the CO. Integrating the first cycle on its own against a flat baseline overstates the area by a quarter.

What CO buys, and what it costs

What it buys is a clean integral. The CO oxidation peak sits by itself in the double-layer region with nothing underneath it — no overlapping second peak, no HER running under one end, and a baseline that is genuinely flat on both sides. Compare that with the Hupd tab, where the baseline choice alone moved the answer by tens of percent. CO also works on alloys and on supported nanoparticles where Hupd is distorted or absent, and the suppression of Hupd in the first cycle is itself the check that the layer was complete.

What it costs is that you no longer know what you counted. Hupd counts Pt atoms: one hydrogen on each, one electron each, and the only constant you need is how many atoms fit in a cm2. CO counts molecules, and how many molecules fit on an atom is the question. Atop CO is one per Pt and strips at 420 µC cm−2; bridged CO is one per two Pt and strips at 210. Divide by 420 regardless and the slider above shows the result: the reported area falls linearly to exactly half the truth as the layer goes from all atop to all bridged, following 1 − x/2. It is an underestimate, never an overestimate, because bridging can only remove molecules.

So use both, and treat the gap as the error bar. The two methods rest on different assumptions — Hupd on a baseline you drew, CO on a binding geometry you assumed — and they fail in different directions, so agreement between them is real evidence and disagreement tells you which assumption broke. On clean polycrystalline Pt they typically land within 10 %. A paper that reports one without the other is reporting a number with no way to check it, which is why the better catalysis papers show the CO strip and the Hupd scan on the same axes.

Only the first two count something. Hupd and CO stripping both end in a number of atoms: a charge, divided by the charge one atom is worth, and the divisor comes from a lattice constant you can look up and check. Capacitance counts nothing. It compares your interface with somebody else's flat one, and Cs for the same material spans 20 to 60 µF cm−2 across the literature — a factor of three sitting in the denominator of every area that rests on it. That is why it is the method of last resort, used where there is no adsorbate to count: oxides, carbides, sulfides, carbons. Report it as a relative area between samples you measured the same way, where Cs cancels, and the method is sound again.

And none of the three is the geometric area. That is the point of the whole lecture: a 5 mm disc has Ageo = 0.196 cm², and the roughness factor above says how many times more Pt than that the electrolyte can actually reach. Divide a current by Ageo and you get a number that depends on how rough your electrode happened to be; divide by the ECSA and you get a specific activity that can be compared with somebody else's. Divide instead by the mass of Pt and you get the mass activity, which is what decides how much platinum a stack needs — im ∝ is × APt,ECSA, so the dispersion is what links the two.

Hupd counts hydrogen on platinum. Most catalysts are not platinum.

What the last few years settled

four results worth knowing before you quote an area

1 · Two monolayers that agree are the measurement; one on its own is a number. A 2025 study replaced the usual sweep with a potential-holding step and then compared two independent probes on the same platinum: the roughness factor came back as 1.93 from Hupd and 1.97 from Cu UPD, two per cent apart. Nothing links those two errors — one is a baseline drawn under hydrogen peaks, the other is a copper layer plated on and stripped off — so their agreement is the evidence. This is why Cu UPD is worth the extra experiment even on a metal where Hupd already works.

2 · Capacitance is worse than the slope makes it look. ECSA = Cdl/Cs is a division, so the whole uncertainty in Cs lands on the area: a perspective on oxygen evolution puts the error from a mis-taken Cs at up to a factor of 103 once it is carried into a per-site turnover. And for poorly conducting nickel oxides and hydroxides the “non-Faradaic” window is not capacitive in the first place, so the Cdl a CV returns is not a measurement of the interface at all. Used as a relative area between samples you prepared and measured the same way, where Cs cancels, it is sound; used as an absolute area it is an order of magnitude.

3 · There is no single ECSA: the area depends on the reaction. Which atoms count as active depends on what you are asking them to do and at what potential — a site that adsorbs hydrogen is not automatically a site that evolves oxygen. So the same electrode has more than one honest area, and an area quoted without its probe is not reproducible. Report the probe, the constant and the electrolyte with the cm², and when you compare two papers, check they counted the same thing before you believe the difference.

4 · One particle at a time: scanning electrochemical cell microscopy. Every number on this page is an average over an electrode. SECCM instead lands a droplet a few hundred nanometres across on one particle and measures only that, then normalises it by the area the droplet actually wetted — taken from the capacitive current inside the droplet, or from a freely diffusing redox couple carried along as an internal standard. On an Fe–N–C oxygen-reduction catalyst that separation mattered: agglomerates of very different size gave the same current density once each was divided by its own ECSA, which an ensemble measurement would have reported as a difference in intrinsic activity.

why the same step can give two slopes

The volcano — Sabatier with a number on it

j0 against the one descriptor, after Trasatti 1972 and Nørskov 2005

The left leg binds too strongly. The surface fills with H and stays full. There is no free site for a Tafel recombination and no empty neighbour for a Heyrovsky step, so whichever finishes the reaction becomes the bottleneck and the current stops responding to potential. Watch θH pin at 100 % as you drag ΔGH left, and watch the slope run away with it.

The right leg binds too weakly. There is almost no adsorbed H to react, so the discharge itself — the Volmer step — is what is slow, and j0 falls off a cliff. Gold and silver sit here; they are not bad metals, they are metals that will not hold the intermediate.

The top is ΔGH ≈ 0. Enough coverage to react and a weak enough bond to let go. Pt, Ir and Rh sit within 0.1 eV of it, and that is the whole of why Pt is the catalyst this lecture has been measuring the area of.

Where the descriptor comes from. H(1s) mixes with the metal d band into a bonding and an antibonding level; the more of the antibonding level that sits below EF and is filled, the weaker the bond. One number, computed, predicting an electrochemical rate — which is why the plot is drawn this way round at all.

Reading a Tafel slope without fooling yourself

three corrections, and one ambiguity that no correction removes

120 mV/dec does not name one step. 2.303RT/αF is the Volmer answer at α = 0.5 — and it is also the Heyrovsky answer once θH has saturated, because a coverage that can no longer rise stops contributing to the potential dependence. Switch the rds pills with ΔGH held strongly negative and watch two different mechanisms give the same number. The slope has to be argued together with a coverage, and the coverage is what the Hupd tab measures.

Subtract iRu first. A Tafel plot that curves upward at high current is uncompensated resistance far more often than it is a change of mechanism — the same Ru that set τ in Lecture 14. At 10 mA over 1 cm² and 10 Ω, 100 mV of your overpotential is not on the electrode at all.

Divide by the real area, not the disc. j0 per geometric area rewards a rough electrode for being rough. Everything on this page — Hupd, CO stripping, Cdl/Cs — exists so that this division can be done, and a volcano plotted on geometric current is a plot of surface areas wearing a catalysis label.

The hydrogen that evolves is not the hydrogen you counted. Hupd is bound too strongly to be the intermediate — it is still there at potentials where no H2 forms at all. The reactive species is the weakly bound, overpotential-deposited H that only exists below 0 V vs RHE, and it is its ΔG that the volcano is drawn against. Hupd still earns its place: it counts the sites that species has to work with, which is the denominator of every current on this tab.

And fit over a decade. A slope read across half a decade of current, near equilibrium where the back reaction still matters, or up where transport is taking over, is a number with no mechanism attached. Fit in the straight part, say where it was, and quote η at 10 mA cm−2 beside it — that one is hard to misreport.
