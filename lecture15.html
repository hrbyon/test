CH40051 Electroanalytical Chemistry · Lecture 15 · Pt catalysis and electrochemically active surface area

Electrocatalysis Bench

A catalyst’s activity is a current divided by an area — and the area is the hard part. The geometric area is not it, the BET area is not it, and what you want is the number of atoms the electrolyte can actually reach. On platinum you can count them: one hydrogen adsorbs on each, one electron each, so a charge becomes an atom count and an atom count becomes square centimetres. Here is that voltammogram, built from the surface waves rather than traced — and then the three measurements that turn it into an area, each played out in the order you would do it: hydrogen stripped off and integrated, carbon monoxide laid on and burnt off against a second cycle, and the double layer of a material with nothing to adsorb at all. And at the end, what the whole count was for: a current per site, a Tafel slope that names the slow step, and the volcano that says why platinum sits on top.

Where the metal itself carries no charge: the pzc

the one potential the double layer has nothing to hold
σM = 0  at  E = Epzc the free charge on the metal, not the charge that crossed — nothing is reacting here
1/C = 1/CH + 1/Cdiff  ·   Cdiff = 228 z√c · cosh(zFη/2RT) µF cm−2 η = E − Epzc. The diffuse layer is thinnest, and so least compliant, when the metal is charged; at the pzc it goes slack and the capacitance falls into a minimum

On either side of it the surface pulls on a different ion. Positive of the pzc the metal holds anions against it — those are the spectators in the surface picture above, sitting on the bare metal through the whole double-layer region. Negative of it the cations come in instead. At the pzc there is nothing to hold: no excess charge on the metal, no diffuse layer to balance it, and the ions are left to the solvent.

The minimum is how it is measured — and why it cannot be, here. In a dilute electrolyte Cdiff is small enough to dominate the series pair and the minimum is sharp; concentrated, the diffuse layer is already compressed and the curve flattens until the minimum is gone. That is the classical measurement, and on platinum it fails for a better reason than dilution: hydrogen and OH are adsorbing over almost the whole window, so what you measure is their charge, not the metal’s. The number has to come from displacing the adsorbed layer with CO at a fixed potential and asking where the displaced charge is zero — about 0.3 V vs RHE on Pt(111) in acid. Which is also why the double layer of this lecture is read between 0.4 and 0.6 V: a window positive of the pzc, where the metal is charged, nothing reacts, and the capacitance is as near constant as platinum ever gets.

And it is why Cs is not one number. A capacitance divided into an area is read at some potential, and it is the distance from that material’s own pzc that sets what the diffuse layer contributes. Comparing Cdl for two materials measured at the same potential on the same scale is comparing two different places on two different curves.

Two hydrogens on the same metal: Hupd and Hopd

one of them is counted, the other one reacts — and they are never the same atoms
H+ + e− + Pt → Pt–Hupd   at  E > 0 V vs RHE stronger than half an H–H bond, so it forms before the potential at which H2 could
ΔEupd = −ΔGads / F 0.13 V of underpotential is 12.5 kJ mol−1 of extra binding; 0.265 V is 25.6 kJ mol−1
H+ + e− + Pt → Pt–Hopd → H2   only at  E < 0 V vs RHE weakly bound, never more than a few percent of the sites, and gone again as fast as it arrives

Hupd is the one you count. A hydrogen atom sitting on platinum is more stable than the same atom in half a molecule of H2, and a stability worth ΔG shifts the potential at which it forms by −ΔG/F — positive of the 0 V where bulk H2 would appear. That is the whole meaning of underpotential: the layer is already finished before the reaction that makes gas has even begun. Two waves, because two kinds of site bind it differently — 0.13 V and 0.265 V of underpotential on this surface. And it saturates: there are only so many sites, one atom each, so the charge to strip it is an atom count and an atom count is an area.

Hopd is the one that reacts. Below 0 V a second, weakly bound hydrogen appears on the sites the strong layer left — and it is this one the Volmer step makes and the Heyrovsky or Tafel step consumes. It never saturates and it cannot be integrated. Its coverage is a steady state between arriving and leaving, so what you measure down there is a rate, not a number of atoms: a current, with gas coming off the electrode into the bargain. This is why the lower limit stays at 0.05 V. Below 0 V no baseline separates the two, and anything integrated there is partly a gas evolution rate.

So the two halves of this lecture are about two different hydrogens. The area comes from Hupd, which is bound too strongly to be the intermediate — it is still sitting there at potentials where nothing evolves. The activity comes from Hopd, and it is its ΔGH that the volcano on the last tab is drawn against. What Hupd contributes to the HER is the denominator: it counts the sites the reaction has to work with.

The two that count atoms land on the truth; the one that counts charge on a surface does not. Hupd and CO are both monolayers with a known number of electrons behind each site, so their divisor is a measurement somebody made. The capacitance divisor is a number you looked up for a material that is not quite yours, and the literature spread of 20–60 µF cm−2 is a factor of three on the answer — which is why it is the method of last resort, used when there is nothing on the surface to count at all.

Only the first two count something. Hupd and CO stripping both end in a number of atoms: a charge, divided by the charge one atom is worth, and the divisor comes from a lattice constant you can look up and check. Capacitance counts nothing. It compares your interface with somebody else's flat one, and Cs for the same material spans 20 to 60 µF cm−2 across the literature — a factor of three sitting in the denominator of every area that rests on it. That is why it is the method of last resort, used where there is no adsorbate to count: oxides, carbides, sulfides, carbons. Report it as a relative area between samples you measured the same way, where Cs cancels, and the method is sound again.

And none of the three is the geometric area. That is the point of the whole lecture: a 5 mm disc has Ageo = 0.196 cm², and the roughness factor above says how many times more Pt than that the electrolyte can actually reach. Divide a current by Ageo and you get a number that depends on how rough your electrode happened to be; divide by the ECSA and you get a specific activity that can be compared with somebody else's. Divide instead by the mass of Pt and you get the mass activity, which is what decides how much platinum a stack needs — im ∝ is × APt,ECSA, so the dispersion is what links the two.

The volcano — Sabatier with a number on it

j0 against the one descriptor, after Trasatti 1972 and Nørskov 2005

The left leg binds too strongly. The surface fills with H and stays full. There is no free site for a Tafel recombination and no empty neighbour for a Heyrovsky step, so whichever finishes the reaction becomes the bottleneck and the current stops responding to potential. Watch θH pin at 100 % as you drag ΔGH left, and watch the slope run away with it.

The right leg binds too weakly. There is almost no adsorbed H to react, so the discharge itself — the Volmer step — is what is slow, and j0 falls off a cliff. Gold and silver sit here; they are not bad metals, they are metals that will not hold the intermediate.

The top is ΔGH ≈ 0. Enough coverage to react and a weak enough bond to let go. Pt, Ir and Rh sit within 0.1 eV of it, and that is the whole of why Pt is the catalyst this lecture has been measuring the area of.

Where the descriptor comes from. H(1s) mixes with the metal d band into a bonding and an antibonding level; the more of the antibonding level that sits below EF and is filled, the weaker the bond. One number, computed, predicting an electrochemical rate — which is why the plot is drawn this way round at all.

Reading a Tafel slope without fooling yourself

three corrections, and one ambiguity that no correction removes

120 mV/dec does not name one step. 2.303RT/αF is the Volmer answer at α = 0.5 — and it is also the Heyrovsky answer once θH has saturated, because a coverage that can no longer rise stops contributing to the potential dependence. Switch the rds pills with ΔGH held strongly negative and watch two different mechanisms give the same number. The slope has to be argued together with a coverage, and the coverage is what the Hupd measurement counts.

Subtract iRu first. A Tafel plot that curves upward at high current is uncompensated resistance far more often than it is a change of mechanism — the same Ru that set τ in Lecture 14. At 10 mA over 1 cm² and 10 Ω, 100 mV of your overpotential is not on the electrode at all.

Divide by the real area, not the disc. j0 per geometric area rewards a rough electrode for being rough. Everything on this page — Hupd, CO stripping, Cdl/Cs — exists so that this division can be done, and a volcano plotted on geometric current is a plot of surface areas wearing a catalysis label.

The hydrogen that evolves is not the hydrogen you counted. Hupd is bound too strongly to be the intermediate — it is still there at potentials where no H2 forms at all. The reactive species is the weakly bound, overpotential-deposited H that only exists below 0 V vs RHE, and it is its ΔG that the volcano is drawn against. Hupd still earns its place: it counts the sites that species has to work with, which is the denominator of every current on this tab.

And fit over a decade. A slope read across half a decade of current, near equilibrium where the back reaction still matters, or up where transport is taking over, is a number with no mechanism attached. Fit in the straight part, say where it was, and quote η at 10 mA cm−2 beside it — that one is hard to misreport.
