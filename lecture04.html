From a voltammogram
C = i / ν   ·   [A] / [V s⁻¹] = [A s V⁻¹] = [C V⁻¹] = [F]

Sweep the potential, read the plateau current. dE/dt is constant on a linear sweep, so the flat top of the box is simply C × ν.

The unit. One farad is one coulomb of charge held per volt applied: 1 F = 1 C V⁻¹. Named for Michael Faraday, and it is an enormous unit — a 1 F capacitor moves a whole coulomb, some 6 × 1018 electrons, for each volt. An electrode this size stores tens of microcoulombs per volt, so double layers are quoted in µF, and — because the number scales with how much metal is wetted — normalised to area as µF cm⁻². That per-area figure is the one worth memorising: 10–40 µF cm⁻² on a metal in aqueous electrolyte.

The measurement

Mercury in sodium fluoride

one metal, one salt, four concentrations — and the pzc never moves

Why this system

neither choice is incidental — together they are what made the double layer measurable
Why mercury.
  • It is a liquid. A dropping electrode renews an atomically smooth, reproducible surface every few seconds. A solid metal carries grain boundaries, steps, oxide and whatever adsorbed on it last — its capacitance depends on the history of the surface, so no two laboratories measure the same number.
  • Its area is known exactly. Capacitance is quoted per unit area, and on a solid electrode the true area exceeds the geometric one by a roughness factor nobody can measure. A drop is a sphere: its area follows from the flow rate and the drop time, so µF cm⁻² means what it says.
  • It is ideally polarizable over an enormous window. Hydrogen evolution on mercury has a huge overpotential, so the electrode can be taken to about −2 V vs SCE with no faradaic current at all. Every microamp measured is double-layer charging — there is no reaction current to subtract, and that is what makes the differential capacitance a clean measurement rather than an inference. The positive end is the limit: mercury itself oxidises past roughly +0.2 V.
  • One uniform surface. A liquid has no crystal facets, so there is a single surface energy and a single pzc — not the facet-by-facet spread a solid gives.
Why fluoride.
  • It will not shed its water. Specific adsorption requires an ion to strip its hydration shell and touch the metal. F⁻ is the smallest halide and so has the highest charge density and the largest hydration enthalpy of them all; the dehydration cost is never repaid by binding to the metal.
  • Hard anion, soft metal. Mercury is polarizable and interacts partly covalently with polarizable anions — which is why the tendency runs F⁻ < PF₆⁻ < BF₄⁻ < ClO₄⁻ < NO₃⁻ < Cl⁻ < Br⁻ < I⁻. Fluoride sits at the bottom because it is hard and barely polarizable. Na⁺ keeps its shell for the same reason.
  • So the interface is electrostatics and nothing else. Gouy–Chapman–Stern assumes exactly that: ions as charges held off at the OHP, interacting through the field alone. NaF is the one common aqueous system that honours the assumption, so it is the control experiment the theory can actually be tested against.
  • Whether the assumption holds is testable, and the curve set at the foot of this page is the test. Run it before reading that card.

What the curve set settles

Where the pzc is, and that nothing adsorbs. The minimum sits at the same potential in every solution — only its depth changes. A feature that ignores how much salt you added belongs to the metal and the solvent, not to the electrolyte, so that invariance is the measurement of Epzc: −0.472 V vs NCE for mercury (−0.43 V vs SCE, −0.19 V vs SHE). It is also the proof that the fluoride argument was right, because specific adsorption would drag the pzc along with the coverage. Repeat this in KCl or KI and the minimum shifts and the positive branch climbs — you would be measuring adsorption chemistry instead of the double layer.
Why the dip fills in. The two capacitors are in series, so the smaller one wins: 1/C = 1/CH + 1/CGC, with CGC = ε₀εrκ·cosh(zFφ₀/2RT). At the pzc the cosh is 1, so CGC = ε₀εr/κ⁻¹ and nothing but the Debye length sets it. Dilute, κ⁻¹ is long and CGC is small enough to dominate — a deep dip. Concentrated, κ⁻¹ collapses, CGC is large, and CH takes the whole measurement.
What it does not explain. The measured NaF curves carry a hump near −0.3 V that Gouy–Chapman–Stern cannot produce, because that model holds CH constant and a constant capacitance has no structure to give. Grahame's own conclusion was that the compact layer's capacitance depends on the charge it carries — the water in it reorients as the field turns over. That is the loose end Bockris, Devanathan and Müller pick up in 1963. Note it is a different mechanism from the anion adsorption above: F⁻ does not adsorb at all, and the hump is there anyway.
