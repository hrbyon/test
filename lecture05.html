The Nernst–Planck equation

deck p.8 — every reason an ion moves, in one line
Ji(x)  =  − Di ∂Ci(x)/∂x   − (ziF/RT) Di Ci ∂φ(x)/∂x   + Ci v(x)
total flux  =  DIFFUSION  +  MIGRATION  +  CONVECTION  ·  mol cm−2 s−1

Three independent reasons an ion arrives somewhere: because there is more of it elsewhere, because a field is pulling it, and because the solvent is carrying it. They add. Move the sliders and watch which one actually decides the answer.
Sign convention: x runs from the electrode surface into the solution, so a negative flux points toward the electrode.

The cell being solved. The electrode at x = 0 is held at the diffusion limit, so every analyte ion reaching it reacts and C(0) = 0; three hundred micrometres away the solution is still bulk.

The stirring. It is taken as a uniform velocity normal to the surface — the stagnant-film idealisation, not a real rotating disk. It is the simplest geometry in which convection does what convection does, and it sets a diffusion layer that stops growing.

The three terms, to scale

Where the equation comes from

deck p.7–8, in four steps
1   a species moves down its electrochemical potential gradient
      ji ∝ |grad μi|   ·   vi = −ki(∂μi/∂x)   ·   ji = Civi

2   write out μi = μi⊖ + RT ln Ci + ziFφ
      ji = −kiCi[ RT ∂lnCi/∂x + ziF ∂φ/∂x ]

3   drop the field term, and C ∂lnC/∂x = ∂C/∂x
      ji ≈ −kiRT (∂Ci/∂x)  ≡  −Di(∂Ci/∂x)   with Di ≡ kiRT — this is Fick 1

4   put the field term back, add the solvent’s own motion
      Ji = −Di∂Ci/∂x − (ziF/RT)DiCi∂φ/∂x + Civ

Two things fall out of step 3 worth keeping. Di = kiRT is the Einstein relation — the same mobility ki governs both diffusion and migration, which is why a Di appears inside the migration term at all. And while C ∂lnC/∂x = ∂C/∂x is exact, using concentration in place of activity is not; that approximation is the foundation the whole of Fick’s law is built on.

From flux to current

deck p.6
−JO(0,t) = i / (nFA)   →   i = −nFA JO(0,t)      itot = id + im + ic

The current is not a separate quantity from the flux — it is the flux of O at x = 0, counted in electrons. That is why the factor is n, the number of electrons each O takes up in O + ne⁻ → R, and not z, the charge on the ion. z belongs to migration, and to the ionic current carried through the solution, i = F Σ ziJi. So the three terms of J are the three terms of itot, and everything on this page is a statement about the current you measure.

At t = 0 the potential is stepped far enough that every O reaching the surface reacts instantly, so C(0,t) = 0. The solution is otherwise untouched and infinitely deep: C(x,0) = C* = 1 mM. That is the one boundary problem the whole of the next lecture is built on.

FICK 1   jd(x,t) = −Di ∂Ci(x,t)/∂x

A snapshot: it converts a slope into a current. Drag the probe and read the tangent — that slope, times −D, is the flux.

0 C* white — depleted: the analyte has been used up at the electrode blue — analyte still there; full colour is the bulk, C*

The strip on top is the solution itself, seen from the side; the blue curve below it is the same colour read off as a number, C/C*. Where the strip fades to white, the curve drops to zero — and as the clock runs, the white region spreads out from the electrode as far as √(2Dt).

Fick 2 — the flux and how fast it changes

j(x,t) and −∂C/∂t, with the slab shaded
FICK 2   ∂Ci(x,t)/∂t = Di ∂2Ci(x,t)/∂x2
how the concentration at a point changes as time passes

Where Fick 2 comes from. Count the molecules in the shaded slab. What enters at x+dx, minus what leaves at x, is what stays behind, so ∂C/∂t = −∂j/∂x; put Fick 1 in for j and that is D ∂2C/∂x2. The green curve is therefore the slope of the blue one: where the blue curve is flat, at the electrode, the green is zero; where the blue falls fastest, at x = √(2Dt), the green peaks. Where the flux drops off fastest is where the solution empties fastest. The bookkeeping card below does the same count with numbers.

blue — |j|, the flux: Fick 1 applied to the profile (left axis) green — −∂C/∂t, the slope of the blue curve, drawn upward because it is a loss: every slab here is losing analyte (right axis) every flux points at the electrode; only the size differs

Where the blue curve comes from. Take the profile from the Fick 1 card, C = C*·erf[x / 2√(Dt)], and apply Fick 1 to it: j = −D ∂C/∂x = −C*√(D/πt)·e−x²/4Dt. Every flux points at the electrode, so only its size |j| is drawn — largest at the surface, where it is the Cottrell current.
