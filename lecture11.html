Electrochemical Kinetics Bench

A current is a rate. Each direction of O + e− ⇌ R has its own rate constant, set by a barrier that the electrode potential tilts — and only a fraction α of the tilt reaches the top. Follow that from the barrier to k° and k, to the exchange current and Butler–Volmer, then read a Tafel plot, including the ways it goes wrong.

Why the current is exponential in the potential

the transition-state picture behind Butler–Volmer
ΔG‡c = ΔG‡0 + αcFη   ·   ΔG‡a = ΔG‡0 − αaFη αa + αc = 1 · η = E − Eeq, anodic positive
k = Af exp(−ΔG‡/RT)  ⇒  i = i0[exp(αaFη/RT) − exp(−αcFη/RT)] Butler–Volmer, if α is constant and the surface sees the bulk

Making the electrode more positive by η lowers the energy of an electron in the metal by Fη, so the O + e− well drops by Fη while the R well stays put. The top of the barrier does not move by the whole of it: only a fraction reaches the transition state, and that fraction is α. Rate is exponential in the barrier and the barrier is linear in η, so the current is exponential in η.

What the picture does not give is the size of i0 — that is the barrier height ΔG‡0, set by the couple and the electrode. The next tab puts a number on it, as k°. What α is, how to read it off the two curves, and what it does and does not control are at the bottom of this tab; press Animate α to watch it change the barrier.

The barrier, tilting

O + e− moves with the potential; R stays put
two wells: O + e− in the metal on the left, R on the right. The potential moves the left-hand one by −Fη; its dashed copy is where it sat at η = 0, and the open ring is the old crossing. the two arrows are the barriers each direction sees, reduction from the left well and oxidation from the right: ΔG‡0 + αcFη and ΔG‡0 − αaFη. hopping dots show the balance, not the magnitude: equal both ways at η = 0, which is i0. Tilt the wells and the traffic goes one way — by the same share whatever α is. Two parabolas, scaled so that the barrier at η = 0 is 0.25 eV whatever α is; their curvature ratio is set by the α you choose, αc = 1/(1 + √(aR/aO)). At α = ½ they are Marcus parabolas with λ = 1.0 eV and ΔG‡0 = λ/4.

The transfer coefficient, α

what the number means — and what it does not
αc = ∂ΔG‡c/∂(Fη)  ·  αa = −∂ΔG‡a/∂(Fη)  ·  αa + αc = 1 the share of a shift that reaches the barrier top; one step, one e−
αc = tanθ / (tanθ + tanφ) θ, φ: slopes of the O + e− and R curves at the crossing
bc = 2.303RT/αcF  ·  ba = 2.303RT/αaF the Tafel slopes are α seen from outside: 118 mV/dec at α = ½, 25 °C

1 · A share of the push. A step of η moves the O + e− well by −Fη. The crossing moves too, but less: by αaFη in the same direction. So the oxidation barrier, measured from the R well that stayed put, changes by −αaFη, and the reduction barrier, measured from the well that moved, by the rest, +αcFη. Both are measured to the same top, which is why the two shares add to one.

2 · Set by the slopes, not by the position. If the R curve is steep and the O + e− curve shallow where they cross, the crossing slides along the steep one and its height hardly changes for reduction: αc is small. Equal slopes give ½. Note what this does not say: at αc = 0.25 these parabolas cross at x* = 0.75, close to R. α is read from the slopes, not from where along the coordinate the crossing sits.

3 · The same number, seen in the double layer. The potential difference between metal and solution falls across the interfacial region. A reactant at the outer Helmholtz plane meets the transition state part-way in, where it feels only part of that drop — the fraction α, which is why α is also called the symmetry factor (Butler’s and Gurney’s picture; Bockris writes β). Both pictures say the same thing: the barrier top sees a fraction of the potential.

4 · What α controls. The Tafel slopes (118 mV/dec at ½; 237 and 79 mV/dec at αc = 0.25); how lopsided the Butler–Volmer curve is; and the reaction orders of i0, 1 − α in O and α in R. Most one-electron outer-sphere couples measure between 0.3 and 0.7, and ½ is the usual assumption when nothing has been measured.

5 · What α does not control. Not Eeq. Not the ratio of the two rates at a given η, which is eFη/RT whatever α is — animate α and the share of hops does not move. Not the small-η slope either: i ≈ i0Fη/RT, because αa + αc = 1. α decides only how the change is shared between the two directions.

6 · Constant only approximately. With curved wells the slopes at the crossing change as it slides, so α drifts with η: for two equal parabolas αc = ½ + Fη/2λ, 0.05 per 100 mV at λ = 1 eV (the local α readout and the right-hand plot). Over the 100–200 mV of a typical Tafel fit this hides in the scatter; it has been seen where kinetics can be followed over a volt (Chidsey, 1991). For a reaction of several steps, the α a Tafel slope gives is an apparent one and can exceed 1 — see the Tafel analysis tab.

Two rate constants, one k°, and where Nernst comes from

the rate constants, and what the concentrations add
kc = k° exp[−αf(E − E°′)]   ·   ka = k° exp[(1 − α)f(E − E°′)] f = F/RT · k° is the value both take at E°′
i = nFA[kaCR(0,t) − kcCO(0,t)] each direction is a rate constant times what is there to react — anodic positive
i = 0  ⇒  Eeq = E°′ + (RT/nF) ln(CO*/CR*) Nernst, out of kinetics: k° and α cancel
i0 = nFAk° CO*(1−α) CR*α the size of either partial current at Eeq; = nFAk°C* when CO* = CR* = C*

Two zeros of potential. k° is defined at E°′, so in the k° form the exponent carries E − E°′. The overpotential η is measured from Eeq. The two are easily written with the same symbol, and they are the same only when CO* = CR*, which is what i0 = nFAk°C* in Butler–Volmer assumes. Move one concentration below and watch the two crossings separate.

k° belongs to the couple on this electrode; i0 to the couple, the electrode and the solution. Change CO* and i0 follows with order 1 − α; change k° and Eeq does not move at all. No mass transport on this tab: the surface sees the bulk concentrations.

One equation, three regions

electron transfer and mass transfer in series
i = i0[eαafη − e−αcfη] Butler–Volmer, surface = bulk · αa = 1 − α, αc = α, f = F/RT
i = i0[eαafη − e−αcfη] / [1 + (i0/il)(eαafη + e−αcfη)] with mass transfer: CO(0)/C* = 1 + i/il, CR(0)/C* = 1 − i/il
|η| small: i ≈ i0fη  ⇒  Rct = RT/nFi0   ·   |η| large: log|i| = log i0 + αaf′η  or  log i0 − αcf′η f′ = F/2.303RT

Two steps in series. The electron has to cross at the surface, and the reactant has to get there. Near Eeq the crossing is the slow step and the current is electron-transfer-limited; far out it is so fast that the surface runs dry and the current becomes mass-transfer-limited, a plateau no potential can raise. Switch mass transport on to see the second.

Signs. Everything here is anodic positive, so the linear region is i ≈ +i0fη. Bard & Faulkner’s figures count cathodic current as positive, which is where the form i ≈ −i0fη comes from — the same curve turned through 180°.

At the surface. The strip above the left plot is the Nernst diffusion layer at the η marked. While CO(0) ≈ CO*, the electrode takes what it is given and the rate is the electron transfer’s. Once C(0) heads for zero the rate is whatever diffusion across the layer can deliver, il = nFm C*, and the potential has nothing more to say.

Which limit comes first is the ratio i0/il. A Tafel line needs back reaction < 1 % (|η| > 118 mV, whatever α is) and depletion < 10 % (|i| < 0.1 il). At α = ½ the current at 118 mV is 10 i0, so the clean stretch is log(il/i0) − 2 decades long; on a branch with transfer coefficient α it is log(il/i0) − 1 − 2α.

What a slope can tell you

an apparent α is not a symmetry factor
hydrogen, if…αcαabc at 25 °Cbc at 60 °C

Volmer H+ + e− → Had; Heyrovsky Had + H+ + e− → H2; Tafel 2Had → H2. If the Volmer step is fast and stays at equilibrium, Had builds up as eF|η|/RT, and a later step that consumes it inherits that potential dependence: once for Heyrovsky (plus half its own electron), twice for the purely chemical Tafel step. Classic low-coverage values with a symmetry factor of 0.5. An α above 1 is therefore not a broken symmetry factor — it says the slow step comes after one or more electrons have already been transferred. A matching slope is evidence for a mechanism, not proof of one.

With one slow step the two add to αa + αc = n/ν (Parsons): n = 2 electrons per H2, and the slow step runs ν times per H2 — twice for Volmer, once for Heyrovsky or Tafel. For a slow Tafel step the oxidation side has αa = 0: it levels off at a kinetic limit instead of climbing. So a fit with 33 mV/dec on both branches, αa = αc = 2, describes the data near η = 0 but cannot come from one slow step — the Pt(110) data on this tab reproduce that fit as reported. A further caution: 2.303RT/2F is also the slope that H2 diffusion alone gives on the evolution side of a reversible electrode, and measurements free of the rotating disk’s transport limit find hydrogen on Pt in acid far faster than the disk shows (Sheng, Gasteiger & Shao-Horn, 2010; Durst et al., 2014).
