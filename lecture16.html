CH40051 Electroanalytical Chemistry · Lecture 16 · Hydrodynamic methods: the rotating disk electrode

RDE and RRDE Bench

Spin the electrode and the current stops decaying. The flow under a rotating disk brings fresh solution up from below and throws it out sideways, and what is left at the surface is a layer of stagnant liquid whose thickness the rotation rate sets — 12 µm at 1600 rpm for a typical ion in water, and the same 12 µm for as long as you keep spinning. Every number here follows from that one layer: the Levich current, which grows as √ω and gives you D from its slope; the profile under the disk, with the Nernst tangent that defines δ and the hydrodynamic layer twenty times thicker around it; and the Koutecky–Levich plot, which separates a slow electrode reaction from the transport that feeds it — and which turns into noise if you read it too close to the plateau.

Read the two halves together. The spinning does not push the reactant into the electrode — it drags solution up the axis and throws it outward across the face, and what that buys you is a thinner stagnant layer. The micron scale on the left does not move when the rate changes, so the band genuinely thins: δ ∝ ω−1/2, four times the rate for half the thickness. On the right the same layer is the distance over which the concentration climbs back to C*, and the slope at the wall — which is the flux, and so the current — is C*/δ. Press Sweep and watch all three figures move together: the band thins, the wave deepens, the Levich point climbs the line.

The disk makes it; the ring counts what escapes

an ORR-like reduction, ring held where H2O2 is oxidised

Mind the signs when you read values off this plot. The disk is reducing, so its current is drawn negative; the ring is oxidising what arrives, so its current is positive. That is the IUPAC convention and it is telling you the direction of each reaction — but N, n and %H2O2 are all defined on the magnitudes. Substitute −1.00 mA for the disk and you will get n > 4, which is the arithmetic telling you a sign was carried where it does not belong.

What the ring cannot tell you

N belongs to the electrode, not to the experiment. It is set by the disk radius and the two edges of the ring — the fraction of what leaves the disk that passes under the ring before the flow throws it outward. Nothing in the chemistry enters, which is why it can be calibrated once, on a couple whose product cannot decompose: Fe(CN)63−/Fe(CN)64−, as in the lecture. Re-polish the disk, or cover the gap with a thick film, and measure it again.

A sequence looks like the direct route. O2 → H2O2 → H2O on the disk and O2 → 2H2O on the disk give the same small ring current, because the ring only ever sees what escaped. Separating them takes rotation rate, not potential: plot iD/iR against ω−1/2 and the intercept holds the branching ratio while the slope holds the rate of the second reduction — Damjanovic's analysis, and the reason an RRDE study quotes several rotation rates.

Peroxide that never arrives is never counted. Decomposition in the gap, or on the catalyst film itself, lowers iR without touching iD. N calibrated on a stable couple is then too large for this species, so n is reported too high and %H2O2 too low. Put the loss slider up and watch a two-electron catalyst be reported as nearly four. An n of 3.9 is not by itself proof of a four-electron catalyst.

And the ring has to be oxidising peroxide, not water. Hold it on the plateau for H2O2 oxidation — about 1.2 V vs RHE in acid — and no higher: above that the ring makes O2 from water or oxidises itself, and every microamp of it is counted as peroxide. The last slider adds exactly that current. Run the blank under argon and subtract it.
