CH40051 Electroanalytical Chemistry · Lecture 9 · Liquid junction and Donnan potentials

Junction & Membrane Bench

Two solutions meet and a voltage appears, with no reaction anywhere. Build the junction and watch the faster ion run ahead until the field it leaves behind holds it back; put a fixed charge inside a membrane and let the salt come to equilibrium around it; then stack the two effects into a pH electrode and find where it stops telling the truth.

A boundary the two solutions cannot agree on

deck p.4–8 — the liquid junction, or diffusion, potential
Elj = φβ − φα = [Σj (|zj|uj/zj)Δcj / Σj |zj|ujΔcj] · (RT/F) ln[Σj|zj|ujcj(α) / Σj|zj|ujcj(β)] Henderson, with Δcj = cj(β) − cj(α)
Elj = ±(RT/F) ln(Λα/Λβ) two different 1:1 salts at the same concentration   ·   Elj = (t+ − t−)(RT/F) ln(cα/cβ) one salt, two concentrations

Nothing reacts at a liquid junction. Both ions of the salt diffuse down the same concentration gradient, but they do not diffuse at the same speed, and the faster one gets ahead. That separates charge — an infinitesimal amount of it, far too little to measure as a concentration — and the field it sets up does two things at once: it pulls the slow ion forward and holds the fast one back. Within nanoseconds — the time ε/σ of Lecture 8, half a nanosecond in 0.1 M KCl — it has grown until the charge the ions carry cancels: every ion is still crossing, and their fluxes are not equal, but Σ zjjj = 0 and no net current flows. That is the steady state (not equilibrium: the salt is still diffusing), and the potential standing across the boundary is the liquid junction or diffusion potential. Only when the two sides hold the same salt does the condition simplify to the cation and the anion crossing at the same rate; with HCl against KCl it is the three fluxes of H+, K+ and Cl− that have to add to zero charge.

It is the one term in a measured cell voltage that no electrode can account for: Ecell = ENernst + Elj. Two calomel electrodes in 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M KCl see the same chloride activity, so the cell ought to read zero — and it reads about 27 mV. That is the junction, and the whole purpose of a saturated KCl salt bridge is to make it small.

How to read it

why a bridge of saturated KCl is the standard answer

1. The sign follows the faster ion. Put 0.1 M HCl against 0.1 M KCl. The only difference between the two sides is H+ against K+, and H+ is nearly five times as mobile. It crosses into the KCl faster than K+ crosses the other way, so the KCl side charges positive and the HCl side is left negative: Elj = φβ − φα comes out about +27 mV, exactly what the voltmeter sees between two identical calomel electrodes.

2. A bridge is two junctions, not one. Putting saturated KCl between the two solutions does not remove the boundary — it replaces one boundary with two, α|KCl and KCl|β, and the meter reads their sum. The picture above is a single junction, so setting β to 4.2 M KCl shows you only the first of the two; the strip below, and the three readouts beside it, do the whole configuration for whatever α and β you have set. The two steps carry opposite signs and largely cancel: 0.1 M HCl against 0.1 M KCl falls from 27 mV to about 3 mV once the bridge is in.

3. Why it works: the bridge drowns the difference. Henderson weights every ion by |z|u c. Flood the junction with 4.2 M KCl and the two bridge ions dominate both sums — and K+ and Cl− have almost the same mobility (t+ = 0.49 against t− = 0.51), so neither runs ahead and there is almost nothing left to separate. The remaining millivolt or two is why a salt bridge reduces Elj rather than removing it. LiCl would be a poor bridge: t+ = 0.34, so two thirds of the current is carried by Cl−.

4. The same salt, two strengths, is the smaller effect. There the two sides share both ions, and only the transference numbers survive: Elj = (t+ − t−)(RT/F) ln(cα/cβ). For KCl that prefactor is 0.49 − 0.51 = −0.02, so a tenfold step gives about 1 mV; for HCl it is +0.64, and the same step gives 38 mV. One junction, two decades of difference, decided entirely by which ions are carrying the current. Mobilities here are infinite-dilution values (Lecture 8), so above about 0.1 M read these numbers as the trend rather than the measurement.

A charge that cannot leave

deck p.9–12 — the Donnan potential, at equilibrium
μ̃iα = μ̃iβ  →  Δφ = φβ − φα = (RT/ziF) ln(aiα/aiβ)   ·   aKαaClα = aKβaClβ the Donnan equilibrium
one potential difference, written from either ion — and they must agree, which is what fixes the concentrations

Now nothing is diffusing. A membrane carries charges that are part of the polymer and cannot move — sulfonate groups in Nafion, quaternary ammonium groups in an anion exchanger, oxide in glass. The small ions can cross freely, so at equilibrium each of them has the same electrochemical potential μ̃ = μ° + RT ln a + zFφ on both sides. That does not make the concentrations equal: an ion can pay for a lower activity inside with a favourable potential. Write Δφ from K+ and again from Cl−, insist the two agree, and the Donnan equilibrium falls out — the ion product is the same on both sides, even though neither concentration is.

The counter-ion is drawn in and the co-ion is pushed out: Donnan exclusion. It is why a cation-exchange membrane passes Li+ and blocks the anion in a battery, why a membrane holding impermeant protein anions already has tens of millivolts across it before any pump runs, and why the glass bulb of a pH electrode can have a potential across it before any diffusion begins.

How to read it

the arithmetic behind the picture

1. Two conditions, one unknown. Electroneutrality holds inside each phase separately — in the solution CK = CCl, in the membrane the mobile ions have to balance the fixed charge as well. The Donnan equilibrium, CKαCClα = CKβCClβ, is the second condition. With the salt shared between two equal volumes they fix every concentration: for the deck’s numbers, 0.1 M KCl against 0.1 M of fixed positive charge, K+ ends at 0.067 M outside and 0.033 M inside, Cl− at 0.067 and 0.133, and Δφ = (RT/F) ln 2 = 17.8 mV.

2. Two bars per ion, and they have to meet. Equilibrium says μ̃ = μ° + RT ln a + zFφ is the same in both phases. Rearranged, that is (RT/F) ln(cα/cβ) = zΔφ for every ion that can cross — and the middle panel draws those two quantities, in millivolts, beside each ion. A gap between the bars is an ion still moving. They close from opposite directions for the cation and the anion, because z flips the sign, and both have to close on the same Δφ. That last requirement is the whole content of the Donnan condition.

3. The same step, written from either ion. K+ says Δφ = (RT/F) ln(aKα/aKβ); Cl−, with z = −1, says Δφ = (RT/F) ln(aClβ/aClα). The readout shows one number because the equilibrium has already forced them to agree — and setting them equal is precisely how the ion-product rule is derived.

4. Salt drowns it, as it drowns everything. Raise the bulk KCl and the fixed charge stops mattering: the mobile ions already outnumber it, the ratio approaches 1 and Δφ collapses. Lower it and the membrane is almost pure counter-ion, the co-ion is excluded nearly completely, and Δφ grows by 59 mV for every tenfold gap. A membrane electrode is therefore only selective while its fixed charge is large compared with the salt it is sitting in — which is the same reason a supporting electrolyte wipes out migration in Lecture 8.

Both effects, stacked into one electrode

deck p.13–21 — the pH glass electrode and its errors
Em = constant + (RT/F) ln(aHα + kpotH,Na aNaα)   ·   kpotH,Na = KH,Na(uNa/uH)
Ecell = L + (0.0592/n) log a 25 °C, so 59.2 mV per decade for a singly charged ion   ·   Nikolsky–Eisenman · the selectivity coefficient is an ion-exchange equilibrium times a mobility ratio

A pH meter is five interfaces in series, and only one of them knows anything about the sample. The two Donnan equilibria at the hydrated faces of the glass set the ion exchange — H+ from the solution for Na+ in the glass — and the diffusion potential through the dry middle of the membrane, where only Na+ and H+ can move, adds the Henderson term of the first tab. Put them together and the membrane potential is the Nikolsky equation: the electrode responds to aH plus a small multiple of aNa.

Everything else in the cell — both reference electrodes, the internal 0.1 M HCl, the bridge junction — is deliberately held constant, and swept into L. That is the whole design: five interfaces, four of them fixed, so the reading is a straight line in pH with a slope of 59.2 mV.

Why the glass has to prefer H+ by 1011

selectivity, and the two errors

The exchange, and the walk across. At each wet face, Na+(α) + H+(m′) ⇌ H+(α) + Na+(m′), with equilibrium constant KH,Na; inside the dry glass the two cations move at different speeds, which is a Henderson term with uNa/uH. The selectivity coefficient is the product of the two: kpot = KH,Na(uNa/uH) ≈ 10−11 for Corning 015 glass (~22 % Na2O, 6 % CaO, 72 % SiO2). The glass must accept H+ and release Na+ readily — a preference of eleven orders of magnitude is what buys a clean reading all the way to pH 9 in salty samples.

Alkaline error. The error sets in above about pH 9 and grows a decade at a time: by pH 11 a sample with 1 M Na+ holds 1011 times more Na+ than H+, which is where a selectivity of 10−11 stops being enough. The glass then answers both, the line flattens, and the meter reads lower than the true pH. Every singly charged cation does this, which is why a “low-sodium” lithium glass is used for strong alkali.

Acid error. Below about pH 0.5 the opposite happens, and it is not in the equation: every exchange site on the surface is already filled with H+, so the glass has nothing left to respond with and the reading comes out higher than the truth. Saturation, not competition — which is why the Nikolsky curve above is drawn only as far as the glass is still honest.

Two ways a potential appears without a reaction

one at steady state, one at equilibrium
liquid junction: different speeds, a steady state, Henderson   ·   Donnan: a fixed charge, true equilibrium, μ̃α = μ̃β
and a glass electrode is both at once, in series

A potential difference in solution can be defined only where nothing is changing: either the system is at equilibrium, or it has reached a steady state. Those are the two cases of this lecture. At a liquid junction the salt is still moving — the potential is what makes the two ions move together. At a charged membrane nothing is moving on balance — every ion still crosses, in and out at the same rate — and the potential is what pays for the uneven concentrations. In both, the charge actually separated is vanishing: a field of 2 × 107 V/m needs so little charge that both phases stay electroneutral to any measurement you could make on them.
