An ion atmosphere that has to keep up

deck p.4–5 — the relaxation and electrophoretic effects
Λm = Λm° − (A + BΛm°)√c   ·   A = zeFκD,1/3πη   ·   B = λBκD,1(2 − √2)/6
κ = (l/A)·(1/R) S m−1, from the cell   ·   Λ = κ/C S m2 mol−1   ·   ΛC = Λ° − k√C Kohlrausch
Debye–Hückel–Onsager, 1:1 salt  ·  electrophoretic term  ·  relaxation term  ·  κD,1 is the Debye κD at 1 mol/L, λB the Bjerrum length
Two different κ on this page. κ alone is the conductivity of the solution, S m−1: what the cell measures, κ = (l/A)(1/R). κD is the Debye parameter, m−1: the inverse thickness of the ion atmosphere, so 1/κD is the Debye length of Lecture 6. They are unrelated quantities that share a letter — one is a property of the whole solution, the other a length around one ion. Both grow with concentration, which is why the confusion is easy: κ roughly as c, κD as √c. Lecture 6 and most textbooks write the Debye one as plain κ; it carries the D here only to keep the two apart.

The Ion Atmosphere of Lecture 6 is a snapshot: an ion at rest, its counter-charge spread symmetrically around it. Switch on a field and the ion moves — and its atmosphere cannot move with it. The cloud is not a shell the ion carries but a statistical excess that has to be torn down behind the ion and rebuilt in front of it, and that takes a time τ of order ε/σ, a few nanoseconds in 10 mM salt. So a moving ion always has more counter-charge behind it than ahead of it, and that lopsided cloud pulls it back: the relaxation, or asymmetry, effect.

At the same time the counter-ions are being driven the other way. They drag their solvent with them, so the ion is swimming upstream: the electrophoretic effect. Both grow with the density of the atmosphere, both go as κD and therefore as √c, and together they are why molar conductivity falls with concentration exactly as Kohlrausch measured — and why that fall is a limiting law, true only as c → 0.

How to read it

the moving picture and the plot, together

1. Why the cloud lags. The atmosphere is rebuilt continuously by diffusion, and that takes τ. In the time τ the ion has already moved on by uEτ, so the centre of its counter-charge sits that far behind it. Press Switch it off: the ion stops, and the cloud slides forward and re-centres over about one τ — that re-centring is the relaxation.

2. Why a tiny lag matters. The real lag is a small fraction of a picometre, far below the Debye length. It still matters because the field it creates is proportional to it, and the lag is itself proportional to E — so the relaxation force is a fixed fraction of the driving force, B√c, at any ordinary field. In 10 mM salt that is about 2 %. Only at fields near 107 V/m does the lag become comparable with the atmosphere, and the conductivity starts to rise (the Wien effect).

3. From one ion to the plot. Both losses are set by the density of the atmosphere, so both go as κD ∝ √c: that is why the measured circles fall on a straight line against √c at low concentration. The line is Onsager’s, with no fitted parameter. Past about 10 mM the circles lift above it — the same failure of point charges that the extended law fixes for activity.

One friction, three numbers

deck p.6–14 — Stokes–Einstein, Nernst–Einstein, Walden
D = kBT / 6πηr   ·   u = |z|e / 6πηr = |z|FD / RT   ·   λ° = |z|Fu = z2F2D / RT
Stokes–Einstein  ·  mobility  ·  Nernst–Einstein

Treat the moving ion as a sphere of radius r in a fluid of viscosity η. Stokes’ drag 6πηrv is then the only thing that resists it — and it resists drift and diffusion alike. That single friction fixes all three numbers at once: change r, η or T and D, u and λ° all move together, in step. Everything below is that one equation, read three ways.

How to read it

what each knob actually changes

1. Radius: 1/r, and it is not the crystal. Halve r and D, u and λ° all double. But r here is the moving sphere: the ion plus the solvent it drags. Li⁺ has the smallest crystal radius of the alkali metals (0.076 nm) and the largest hydrodynamic one (0.238 nm), because its charge sits on a small surface and holds its water tightly. That is why Li⁺ is the slowest of Li⁺, Na⁺ and K⁺ — the opposite of what the crystal radii suggest.

2. Temperature works through η. D goes as T/η, and of the two it is the viscosity that moves. From 25 to 45 °C the absolute temperature rises 7 %, but water thins by a third, so D rises by about 60 %. Warm a cell and everything speeds up — diffusion, migration and conductivity together, by the same factor.

3. Where it breaks. Stokes’ law treats the solvent as a continuum, which it is not on the scale of one ion: below about 0.15 nm — the size of a water molecule — r is only an effective number for ranking ions. And H⁺ and OH⁻ ignore the law altogether: they hop along hydrogen bonds instead of pushing through the fluid, which is why their λ° is several times larger than any sphere of their size could give.

Which ions carry the current

deck p.15
t+ = i+/(i+ + i−) = λ+/(λ+ + λ−) 1:1 salt   ·   ti = |zi|Ciλi / Σk|zk|Ckλk any mixture   ·   Σ ti = 1

Cations and anions both carry the current, but not equally: each carries a share in proportion to how many of it there are, its charge, and how fast it moves. That share is its transference number. In HCl the proton is four and a half times as mobile as chloride and carries 82 % of the current; in LiCl the slow, well-hydrated Li⁺ carries only a third. Add an inert salt in excess and it takes over the current, leaving almost none for the analyte — which is exactly what a supporting electrolyte is for (Lecture 5).
