A solvent is not a passive background. Boiling point and vapour pressure set the liquid range and how fast it leaves the cell; viscosity sets how fast ions move, and so the conductivity; δ says what will dissolve; εr divides every electrostatic force between ions. But εr says nothing about which ion the solvent wraps itself around — that is what the donor number and acceptor number measure, and the pairing rule is HSAB: a small hard cation like Li⁺ is held by a high-DN solvent, small hard anions like F⁻ and OH⁻ by a high-AN, hydrogen-bond donating one. The three plots below take these one pair at a time.

Reading the three plots

dashed: the least-squares line through the points on show

(1) Does a big dipole make a big εr? Mostly yes: nitriles, amides and carbonates carry big dipoles and big εr. The exceptions go one way, for one reason. Water’s dipole is only 1.77 D, smaller than acetonitrile’s 3.53 D, yet its εr is more than twice as large, and N-methylformamide reaches 182 on 3.86 D. The Debye equation treats each molecule as turning on its own; hydrogen bonds line neighbouring dipoles up so a whole chain turns together. Furthest above the trend now: —. Where no chain can form — benzene, hexane, dioxane — εr collapses to about 2.

(2) Boiling point and viscosity both measure how hard the molecules hold on to one another, so they rise together. Hydrogen-bonded liquids (formamide, the acids) and strongly dipolar ones (sulfolane: 287 °C and 10.3 cP; EC, PC) sit at the top right; small, weakly held molecules (hexane, acetone, DCM, THF, DME) are volatile and runny. That is the electrolyte designer’s trade-off: the high-εr carbonates that dissociate a salt are viscous and slow its ions, the fluid ethers evaporate — so real electrolytes are mixtures. Size spoils the match: a big molecule boils high without flowing slowly.

(3) AN against DN: which ion is held. High DN, low AN — HMPA, pyridine, DMSO, DMF — wrap cations and leave anions bare and reactive, which is why SN2 rates climb in DMSO. High AN — water, formic and acetic acid, methanol — hold small anions by hydrogen bonds. Both low — hexane, benzene, DCE — solvate nothing, so salts stay paired. Neither number can be read off εr: across these solvents r(DN, εr) = — and r(AN, εr) = —.
