Ion Interactions Bench

No ion in a real solution is alone. Watch the atmosphere of counter-charge that every ion carries thin out as you dilute, count what a Gaussian sphere around it encloses, and follow the same physics to a charged electrode, where Poisson–Boltzmann gives the double layer and its capacitance.

The ion atmosphere

deck p.2–3 — no ion is ever alone
I = ½ Σ ci zi2    ·    1/κ = √( ε0εr kBT / 2NAe2I )
ionic strength  ·  Debye length, the thickness of the atmosphere

Coulomb’s law does not switch off because a solution is stirred. Every cation drags a time-averaged excess of anions around with it, and every anion an excess of cations — the ion atmosphere. It is not a shell of particular ions; it is a statistical bias, rebuilt continuously as the central ion moves, which is why the atmosphere always lags behind and drags: the relaxation or asymmetry effect.

Its thickness is the Debye length 1/κ. Note what is in it and what is not: concentration and charge through I, the solvent through εr, temperature — but nothing about which salt. Two solutions of equal ionic strength screen equally. Why the atmosphere has exactly this thickness is Gauss’s law — the two cards beside this one, and the card below.

Where 1/κ comes from: Gauss’s law, read off the two figures

deck p.11
∮ E·dA = Qenc / ε0εr   ·   ∇·E = ρ / ε0εr = κ2φ   ·   Qenc(r) = zε (1 + κr) e−κ(r−a) / (1 + κa)
the field through a closed surface counts the charge inside  ·  the same law at a point, with Boltzmann’s charge  ·  what a sphere around one ion encloses

1. The law. The total field passing out through any closed surface depends only on the charge inside it. For a sphere of radius r around the ion the field is the same all over it, so E·4πr2 = Qenc/ε0εr. A bare ion keeps Qenc constant, and the field falls as 1/r2 for ever.

2. Read it off the figures. The green circle in The Gaussian sphere and the green dashed line in What the sphere encloses are the same sphere. The 24 field lines stand for the ion’s charge; a line that meets counter-charge inside the sphere ends there (the faint ones), and the rest cross it (orange dots). So lines crossing ÷ 24 = Qenc/zε, the height of the green curve at r.

3. Why the answer is 1/κ. Grow the sphere with ▶. At r = a all 24 cross; as the sphere swallows the atmosphere the curve falls, fastest at the shaded hump’s peak, r = 1/κ; a few Debye lengths out, ion and atmosphere cancel and nothing crosses. Put Boltzmann’s charge into Gauss’s law at every point and it becomes ∇·E = κ2φ — the distance over which the field dies is 1/κ, set by I, εr and T.

How to read the graph

what ψ(r)/ψ(a) against r is showing

What is plotted. The electric potential ψ around one ion, against the distance r from its centre. The vertical axis is that potential divided by its value at r = a, so both curves start at 1.0 and the graph compares how quickly the potential dies away, not how big it is. The grey strip, r < a, is the region no other ion can enter: a is the distance of closest approach — the hydrated ion plus the ion coming up to it — so the atmosphere, and both curves, begin only at a.

The two curves. Grey dashed is the ion alone in the solvent, Coulomb's ψ ∝ 1/r: it only halves each time the distance doubles, and never really goes away. Blue is the same ion inside its atmosphere. The counter-charge gathered around it cancels the field further out, which multiplies the potential by e−κ(r−a). The gap between the curves is the screening.

The marker. The orange line stands at r = a + 1/κ, one Debye length beyond the closest-approach distance. There the atmosphere has cut the potential to 1/e — 37 % of what the bare ion would give — and that distance is what “the thickness of the atmosphere” means. Further out the blue curve falls to nothing while the grey one is still large.

An example. Take an ion 0.5 nm across in a solution concentrated enough that 1/κ = 0.28 nm. The Debye length is shorter than the ion, so the atmosphere is packed against its surface: by r = 0.8 nm the bare potential is still at 0.63 of its surface value, but the screened one has fallen to 0.21. Beyond about 1.2 nm the ion can hardly be felt at all. Now dilute to 1 mM: 1/κ becomes 9.6 nm, far past the right-hand edge, and the two curves lie almost on top of each other — on the scale of a few ion diameters the atmosphere is too far away to matter, and the ion behaves as if it were alone.

The same law at a flat electrode

PB_EDL deck, p.1–3
∇·(εE) = ρ  →  ∇2φ = −ρ/ε0εr   ·   n± = n0 e∓zeφ/kBT   ·   d2φ/dx2 = (2n0ze/ε0εr) sinh(zeφ/kBT)
Gauss, then Poisson  ·  Boltzmann closes it  ·  the Poisson–Boltzmann equation for a z:z salt

Gauss's law gives the potential from the charge. The trouble is that in an electrolyte the charge is not given: the ions arrange themselves according to the very potential you are trying to find. φ needs ρ, and ρ depends on φ — the problem is self-consistent, and it has to be closed before it can be solved.

Boltzmann closes it. At equilibrium the electrochemical potential is flat, so an ion's concentration follows e−zeφ/kBT — counter-ions pile up where the potential attracts them, co-ions thin out. Putting that into Poisson gives one nonlinear equation for φ, and everything on this tab is either a solution of it or an admission about what it left out: point ions, a mean field with no ion–ion correlation, a uniform εr, an ideal solution and no specific adsorption.

Solving it, in two integrations

deck p.4–5
linear   φ = φ0 e−x/λD,   λD = √(ε0εrkBT / 2n0z2e2)
exact   tanh(zeφ/4kBT) = tanh(zeφ0/4kBT) e−x/λD
Grahame   σ = √(8ε0εrkBTn0) sinh(zeφ0/2kBT)

Multiply the PB equation by 2dφ/dx and it integrates once, with φ and φ′ both going to zero far away; that first integral is Grahame's equation in disguise, since Gauss's law at the surface says σ = −ε0εrφ′(0). Integrate again and you have the exact profile above.

At small φ0 the tanh cancels and the exact solution is the exponential — and λD here is the same κ−1 as the thickness of the ion atmosphere on the previous tab. At large φ0 the potential falls far faster than any exponential near the wall: the counter-ions crowd in and screen more efficiently than a linear theory can.

Where it stops working

deck p.8–9 — and why modern electrolytes need more

Concentrated. At 1 M λD is 0.30 nm in water, about one hydrated ion across, so the layer is thinner than the particles it is made of. Above roughly 1 M the measured screening length is reported to grow again — underscreening, still argued over.

Crowded. Point ions can be packed without limit; real ones saturate. The Bikerman and Kornyshev modified PB adds lattice-gas entropy with ν = 2a3n0, which caps the surface concentration and turns C into camel- and bell-shaped curves, as seen in ionic liquids.

Correlated, and not uniform. Multivalent ions and ionic liquids overscreen: the charge oscillates in layers rather than decaying smoothly. And near the electrode the field is strong enough to saturate the solvent, so εr is far below its bulk value right where the potential changes fastest.

A poor solvent makes all of this worse at once: λD goes as √εr, so 1 M salt in a glyme has a nominal λD near 0.1 nm — and by then the salt is ion-paired anyway, so the ionic strength that goes into the formula is not the concentration you weighed out. Read PB in that regime as a qualitative guide, not a calculation.
