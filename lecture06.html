Potential and field, defined

E and ψ side by side: what each one is, and how they differ
ψ = U / q   ·   E = F / q   ·   E = −dψ/dx  ⇔  ψ(x) = ∫x∞ E dx

The potential ψ is a scalar: one number at each point, like a height on a map. The field E is how steeply that potential changes through space — its slope — and it points downhill. It is the field that pushes on a charge; the potential gives the charge’s energy. Around one ion both fall off with distance; between two electrodes you set a voltage, but every ion in between answers to the field, E = ΔV/d.

How to read the two pictures

height, slope, push

1. Potential is a height. ψ is one number at each place, so it can be drawn as shading and joined up into equipotentials, like contour lines. Only differences in ψ matter: moving a charge q from one line to another changes its energy by qΔψ, whatever path it takes.

2. Field is the slope. E = −dψ/dx: steep where the equipotentials crowd, gentle where they spread. Around one ion ψ ∝ 1/r but E ∝ 1/r2, so the field dies away faster than the potential; switch the atmosphere on and both are cut off beyond about 1/κ. Between two electrodes ψ falls in a straight line, so E = ΔV/d is the same everywhere — halve the gap and the field doubles. Fill the gap with an electrolyte and that stops being true: see Electrochemical cell below.

3. The field pushes. F = qE. A positive charge is pushed down the slope, toward lower ψ; a negative one is pushed up it. Both lose energy, because U = qψ falls either way. In solution an ion does not accelerate for long: friction sets a drift speed proportional to E — the start of ionic conductivity in Lecture 8.

Migration: the field is the driving force

why ions move through the bulk, and why they stop
F = zieE   ·   vi = uiE   ·   Jmig,i = −(ziF/RT) Dici dψ/dx = (ziF/RT) DiciE
the force on an ion  ·  its drift speed, with mobility ui = |zi|eDi/kBT  ·  the migration term of the Nernst–Planck flux

1. The push is the field. An ion of charge zie feels F = zieE. In water, friction balances that force within picoseconds, so the ion does not keep accelerating: it drifts at v = uiE, cations along E and anions against it. The migration flux is proportional to −dψ/dx — the slope of the potential, not the potential itself.

2. No slope, no migration. With only non-Faradaic processes, ions migrate only until the double layers are charged. Then the whole voltage sits across a few nanometres at each electrode, the bulk is flat, E = 0 there, and migration through the bulk stops — although the electrodes are still ΔV apart. The same field, concentrated in the double layer, is what holds the counter-ions against diffusion there.

3. Keep a current, keep a field. A Faradaic reaction at the electrodes, or AC too fast for the double layers to finish charging, keeps a field E = j/σ in the bulk, and migration carries the current. That is the field conductivity is measured in: j = σE with σ = Σ|zi|Fuici is Ohm’s law, migration summed over every ion — the subject of Lecture 8.

The ion atmosphere

deck p.2–3 — no ion is ever alone
1/κ = √( ε0εr kBT / 2NAe2I )
Debye length, the thickness of the atmosphere

Every cation drags a time-averaged excess of anions around with it, and every anion an excess of cations — the ion atmosphere. It is not a shell of particular ions; it is a statistical bias, rebuilt continuously as the central ion moves. The rebuilding takes time, so a moving ion leaves more of its counter-charge behind than it gathers ahead. The atmosphere’s charge distribution becomes asymmetric, and that lopsided counter-charge pulls the ion backward by electrostatic attraction: the relaxation or asymmetry effect.

Its thickness is the Debye length 1/κ. Note what is in it and what is not: concentration and charge through I, the solvent through εr, temperature — but nothing about which salt. Two solutions of equal ionic strength screen equally. Why the atmosphere has exactly this thickness is Gauss’s law — the cards beside this one.

Where 1/κ comes from: Gauss’s law, read off the two figures

deck p.11

1. Gauss’s law. Count the field lines that cross a sphere drawn around the ion: the count tells you the charge inside it, Qenc. A bare ion has nothing around it, so all 24 lines cross, however big the sphere.

2. Add the atmosphere. It carries the opposite charge. As the sphere grows it takes in more of it, so more lines end inside and fewer cross. ① r = a: all 24 cross. ② r = 1/κ: the count falls fastest. ③ about 6/κ out: none cross.

3. So what is 1/κ? The distance over which the atmosphere cancels the ion’s charge. Beyond a few 1/κ the ion is hidden. More salt packs the atmosphere closer, so 1/κ gets shorter.

What r = 1/κ means (②). It is a distance measured from the ion’s centre: the radius of the thin shell that holds the most counter-charge. It does not ignore a. The charge in a shell from r to r + dr is −dQenc/dr = ze·κ2·r e−κr·eκa/(1 + κa), for r ≥ a. The closest approach a only decides where the atmosphere starts and scales the whole curve by a constant; the shape, r e−κr, is a charge density that falls away from the ion multiplied by a shell area 4πr2 that grows, and it peaks at r = 1/κ whatever a is. (When 1/κ < a that peak would lie inside the closest approach, so the most charge sits at r = a.) In dilute solution a ≪ 1/κ, so 1/κ and a + 1/κ are almost the same.
