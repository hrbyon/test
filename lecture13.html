Cyclic Voltammetry Bench

One triangular sweep, and the current that comes back carries the thermodynamics, the electron-transfer rate, the diffusion coefficient and whatever chemistry the product goes on to do — all tangled together in one trace. This page untangles them one condition at a time. Every voltammogram here is simulated, not drawn: the diffusion equation is solved at each step, so the shapes are consequences, not illustrations.

A triangle in, a peak back — and the peak is the whole point

the four equations this page is built on
E(t) = Ei − υt, reversed at Eλ the only thing you control — and υ is the clock the whole experiment is timed against
ip = 0.446 nFAC*√(nFDυ/RT) = (2.69×105) n3/2 A D1/2 C* υ1/2 Randles–Sevcik, reversible · the irreversible form carries 2.99×105√α instead
mT = D/δ ≈ √(DFυ/RT)  ·   Λ = k°/mT the sweep sets how fast mass transport is, so υ decides which of k° and mT is the slow one
ΔEp = Ep,a − Ep,c → 59/n mV  ·   |ip,a/ip,c| → 1 the two reversibility tests — and both of them fail in their own characteristic way

Why a peak and not a plateau. Walk the potential down and two things happen at once. The Nernst condition drags the surface ratio CO/CR down exponentially, which drives the current up; and every electron taken costs one molecule of O from a layer that nothing is replenishing, which drives the current down. The first wins early, the second wins late, and the crossing is the peak. Nothing about the peak is a property of the molecule alone.

Why √υ and not υ. The diffusion layer grows as δ ≈ √(Dt), and the experiment lasts t ≈ RT/Fυ. Sweep ten times faster and the layer is √10 times thinner, so the gradient — and the current — is √10 times steeper. But the sweep is also over in a tenth of the time, so the charge goes the other way: Q ≈ ipt ∝ √υ/υ = 1/√υ. Ten times the scan rate passes √10 times less charge — which is the same statement as the thinner layer, since Q = nFAC*δ is just the material taken out of it. A fast sweep is a smaller experiment, not a bigger one, and that is why a CV can be run repeatedly on the same drop.

Look at what does not change. Ten times the scan rate gives √10 = 3.16 times the peak current, and leaves Ep exactly where it was. A peak potential that does move with υ is the single most useful diagnostic in the whole of voltammetry: it means either the electron transfer cannot keep up, or the product is reacting. ‘Reversible’ is not a property of the molecule

it is a verdict on k° compared with the scan rate
Λ = k° / √(DFυ/RT) one dimensionless number decides the shape — everything else is cosmetic
Λ > 15: Nernst holds at the surface → ΔEp = 59/n mV, fixed  ·   0.3 < Λ < 15: quasi-reversible → ΔEp grows with υ  ·   Λ < 0.3: irreversible → no return peak at all and because Λ ∝ υ−1/2, the same couple moves between all three as you turn the scan rate up

The deck puts it as a race. mT = D/δ is how fast mass transport can deliver; k° is how fast the interface can take. Sweeping faster thins δ, which speeds mass transport up — so the electron transfer is the one left behind. This is why the deck's ‘higher scan rate shows better reversibility’ is exactly backwards for electrode kinetics, and right only for outrunning chemistry, which is the next tab.

Nicholson’s working curve

ΔEp against Λ
The curve — ΔEp as a function of Λ, recomputed here by the same simulator that draws the trace rather than copied out of the 1965 table, so that it can be checked. 57 mV — the limit, 2.218RT/nF. The curve settles onto it from above and never crosses it. What you measure is a little more than this, and the panel at the foot of this tab says why. you are here — the Λ and the ΔEp of the trace beside it, dropped onto the curve. Λ < 0.3 — irreversible. The return peak has gone, so there is no ΔEp to measure at all; the curve is drawn pinned to the top of the frame to say so, not because ΔEp is really 320 mV. Λ > 15 — reversible. The curve is flat: ΔEp has stopped depending on k°, so measuring it carries no kinetic information. Only the unshaded middle returns a k°, which is the whole reason the quasi-reversible window matters.

How it is used backwards. You measure ΔEp, find it on the vertical axis, read Λ off the horizontal one, and multiply: k° = ΛmT, with mT = √(DFυ/RT). That is the whole of Nicholson’s method. It assumes α = 0.5 and DO = DR — break either with the sliders and the Λ you read back is wrong, while the picture still looks perfectly respectable.

The trap in E1/2. The midpoint of the two peaks is only E°′ when DO = DR; in general it is offset by (RT/nF)ln√(DR/DO). Try the last preset: the whole wave slides negative without a single thermodynamic quantity having changed.

57 or 59? The gap is the switching potential

ΔEp → 2.218RT/nF, but only in the limit

Where 2.218 comes from. Nernst at the surface plus semi-infinite diffusion collapses the whole reversible sweep to one convolution equation with no free parameters in it, ∫0sg(z)/√(s−z)dz = 1/(1+θ), and that equation has no closed-form solution — it has to be integrated numerically, which is what Randles and Ševčík did in 1948. One curve then gives three numbers at once: its maximum is ψp = 0.4463, the Randles–Sevcik constant; that maximum sits at Ep − E1/2 = −1.109RT/nF = −28.5/n mV; and its half-height width is 2.20RT/nF. 0.4463 and 1.109 are the y and the x of the same point — neither is derived, both are read off the same solution. Turn the sweep around far enough and the anodic peak is that point's mirror image at +1.109RT/nF, and the separation between them is 2 × 1.109 = 2.218RT/nF = 57.0/n mV.

And why the trace never shows it. That mirror is exact only if you reverse infinitely far past the peak. At any real switching potential the anodic peak has not finished developing when you catch it, and the separation comes out wider:

So carry 59/n, and know what it is. Reverse 100–300 mV past the peak, which is what almost every real experiment does, and a perfectly reversible couple gives 58–59/n mV. That is why this page, the deck and most textbooks quote 59/n, why some quote 58.3, and why the rigorous 57.0/n appears in none of your data: it is where the number is heading, not what you will see. The dashed green line on the working curve above is drawn at 57 for exactly this reason — the computed curve settles onto it from above, and the ΔEp readout beside it says 58.

The useful consequence. A couple of mV of this is switching potential and nothing else. If ΔEp is well above 60 mV, that is k° — and the working curve above turns it into a number. Reversing further will not fix it, and neither will reversing less: try it with the switching slider and watch ΔEp move by two or three mV while Λ moves it by two hundred.

A straight line through the origin, or an excuse

what ip vs √υ is actually testing
ip = (2.69×105) n3/2 A D1/2 C* υ1/2 A in cm2, D in cm2 s−1, C* in mol cm−3, υ in V s−1 → ip in A
slope = (2.69×105) n3/2 A D1/2 C* so one plot gives D if n is known, or n if D is · and a curved plot says the premise is wrong

The equation assumes three things, and the plot tests all three at once: the only transport is diffusion (no stirring, no migration, no adsorption), the species is dissolved and free (so the amount reacting grows as √υ rather than staying fixed), and the kinetics are fast enough not to matter. Break the third and the line bends down at high υ; break the second — an adsorbed monolayer — and ip goes as υ, not √υ.

Six scan rates, one couple

—

The current axis is held fixed on purpose. If it were refitted to the data every time you moved a slider, ten times the concentration would redraw an identical picture with different numbers beside it — and the thing worth seeing, that the height answers to the conditions, would be invisible. It behaves like a current range on an instrument instead: it holds where it is, and steps by 1–2–5 only when the trace would leave the frame or shrink into the baseline. Nothing here is divided by √υ, so a fiftyfold scan rate should give √50 ≈ 7 times the peak. Watch the diffusion tail past the peak as well: it grows with it, because the whole diffusion layer is thinner.

ip against √υ

the diagnostic plot

Same current axis as the trace above, and held fixed for the same reason: the six dots are the six peaks, read straight off those voltammograms, so a height here and a height there are one quantity. The grey line is a least-squares fit forced through the origin, because the equation has no intercept; the faint green one is what the equation predicts from the n, A, C* and D that went in. Now drag k° down. The points do not leave the line — R² hardly moves — the line itself tilts away from the predicted one, because slow kinetics pull every peak down together. That is the trap worth learning: a plot can be straight, through the origin, with R² = 0.999, and still return a D that is a third too small. Straightness proves diffusion control, nothing more; reversibility is the previous tab’s question.

The product does not always sit there waiting to be put back

homogeneous reactions coupled to the electron transfer
K1 = k1(RT/F)/υ  ·   K2 = k2C*(RT/F)/υ the chemistry, measured against the sweep — this is what a scan rate study changes
K < 0.1: outrun it → a clean reversible couple  ·   K ≈ 1: the diagnostic window  ·   K > 10: the return peak is gone and Ep has moved so a mechanism is assigned from how the trace changes with υ, never from one voltammogram

Why the forward peak moves positive. Removing B pulls the surface equilibrium over — Le Chatelier at an electrode. The couple reaches the same ratio at a less negative potential, so a reduction becomes easier the faster the follow-up chemistry runs. Ten times slower a sweep, ten times more chemistry per sweep, and the peak moves about 30 mV positive.

Why it stops working when the kinetics are slow. If Λ << 1 the rate-determining step is already the electron transfer; whatever happens to B afterwards cannot reach back through it. The deck's Λ = 0.1 panels show exactly this — Ep stops responding to K altogether, and the diagnosis has to come from somewhere else.
